## The problem: you've done everything the lists tell you to do

You've done the checklist. You're pumping after every feed. You tried power pumping. You're drinking plenty of water, upping your protein, taking moringa and fenugreek. Your supply was fine at first—then it tanked. And now you're wondering if the insulin resistance that came with your PCOS diagnosis is part of the problem.

It turns out insulin resistance *does* affect milk production. The question is whether metformin—the medication often prescribed for PCOS and insulin resistance—can help.

## Why insulin resistance affects lactation

Insulin doesn't just regulate blood sugar. It plays a direct role in breast development and milk synthesis. The receptor cells in breast tissue need to stay insulin-sensitive to respond properly to prolactin and other lactation hormones. When you have insulin resistance, those receptors don't work as well—and that can interfere with both the initial "milk coming in" phase (lactogenesis II) and ongoing milk production.

Women with PCOS are more likely to experience low milk supply, and [research suggests the link runs through insulin resistance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8992687/). One study found that women with signs of insulin resistance had significantly lower milk production at baseline than those without those signs. Obesity—common in PCOS and tied to insulin resistance—compounds the issue, affecting both breast development during puberty and lactation later on.

So the biology checks out. Insulin resistance can interfere with milk supply. The harder question is whether treating it postpartum makes a difference.

## What the metformin research actually shows

Metformin is sometimes used off-label as a galactagogue—a substance meant to increase milk production. The idea makes sense: if insulin resistance is part of the problem, improving insulin sensitivity should help. But the evidence is thin.

The only randomized controlled trial to date was small—just 15 women total, with 10 in the metformin group and 5 receiving placebo. The results trended in the right direction: women assigned to metformin saw a median increase of 8 mL per day at their peak, compared to a *decrease* of 58 mL in the placebo group. But the difference wasn't statistically significant. With such small numbers, even a couple of responders or non-responders can swing the percentages dramatically—so while only 2 of the 10 women on metformin sustained improved output through day 28, that's hard to interpret meaningfully. About 4 to 5 of the 10 reported nausea or vomiting, a common metformin side effect, and none of them felt the drug was worth continuing after the study ended.

The [NIH's LactMed database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501020/)—the authoritative reference on drugs and breastfeeding—notes that metformin passes into breast milk in very low amounts (typically less than 0.5% of the maternal dose reaches the infant) and is considered safe during lactation. But it also states plainly: "Evidence does not show effectiveness" as a galactagogue.

The [Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine's clinical protocol on galactagogues](https://www.bfmed.org/assets/DOCUMENTS/PROTOCOLS/9-galactogogues-protocol-english.pdf) emphasizes that before reaching for any medication or supplement, you need to rule out other causes—inadequate milk removal, anatomical issues, thyroid problems, retained placenta—and maximize non-pharmacologic management first.

## What this means if you're considering it

If you have PCOS, insulin resistance, and persistently low supply despite doing everything right with pumping and feeding frequency, [metformin *might* be worth discussing with your provider](/articles/should-you-add-myoinositol-to-metformin-for-pcos/)—but it's not a sure bet. The safety profile during breastfeeding is solid, so the risk to your baby is low. The bigger issue is that it may not work, and the GI side effects (nausea, diarrhea, stomach cramps) can be rough when you're already exhausted postpartum.

Before you go that route, make sure you've actually addressed the mechanical and hormonal basics. Have you worked with a lactation consultant who assessed latch, transfer, and your full feeding routine? Have they checked for tongue or lip ties in your baby? Has your provider ruled out thyroid issues, anemia, or retained placental fragments—all of which can tank supply?

[The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2021/02/breastfeeding-challenges) a thorough lactation assessment and evaluation for medical causes before considering galactagogues. If those boxes are checked and insulin resistance still seems like the missing piece, metformin is a reasonable thing to try—but go in with realistic expectations. It's not a magic fix, and for many people it won't move the needle enough to matter.

## The takeaway

Insulin resistance can interfere with milk production, and that connection is real. Metformin is safe to take while breastfeeding and may help a subset of people—but the evidence so far is underwhelming, the side effects are common, and most people in the one small trial didn't find it worth continuing. If you're already doing the pumping, the hydration, the galactagogue supplements, and the feeding frequency—and supply is still low—it's worth talking to your provider about whether metformin makes sense *for you*. Just don't expect it to be the answer on its own.
