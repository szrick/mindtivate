## Why under-eye Botox can backfire

You loved the crow's feet results, so when a new injector suggested under-eye Botox, it seemed like a natural next step. But now you've got more creases than before, and you're staring down a string of important events wondering if you're stuck looking worse until it wears off.

You're not imagining it, and you're not doomed. What happened is a known complication of periorbital injectables—the area around your eyes is tricky, and even experienced injectors can misjudge placement or dosage.

Botox works by temporarily relaxing muscles. When it's injected into the orbicularis oculi (the muscle that circles your eye), the goal is usually to smooth crow's feet at the outer corners. But the under-eye area is different. [The skin there is extremely thin](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4924418/), and the muscle sits right underneath it. If Botox relaxes the wrong part of that muscle—or relaxes it too much—you lose some of the natural support that keeps the skin taut. The result? More visible creases, a slightly hollow look, or what some people describe as looking tired or older.

Another possibility: if the injector placed Botox too low or too close to the cheek, it can affect your smile mechanics. When you smile or squirm your face naturally, and the muscle movement is suddenly uneven, the skin bunches differently than it used to. That can create new lines where there weren't any before.

## How long you're actually dealing with this

You got the injections in September. Botox typically peaks around two weeks post-injection and lasts three to four months. By November—your Germany trip—you'll be about two months out, so you're likely still in the full-effect window. By December, when you're graduating and heading to the Dominican Republic, you'll be at the three-month mark. Most people start seeing movement return around then, and the appearance that bothered you should begin improving.

It's not instant, but it's also not permanent. The muscle will regain function as the Botox metabolizes, and your under-eye area will return to its baseline.

## What can help in the meantime

You can't reverse Botox, but you can temporarily improve the appearance of the creases while you wait.

Hydrating under-eye patches—especially those with hyaluronic acid and peptides—can plump the skin temporarily. Hyaluronic acid is a humectant, meaning it pulls water into the skin, which smooths out fine lines for a few hours. Peptides may support skin barrier function and give a subtle firming effect. These won't fix the underlying issue (the muscle relaxation), but they can make the area look less crepey for a short window—useful if you're prepping for photos or an event.

Keep the skin well-moisturized daily. A good eye cream with ceramides or niacinamide helps maintain the skin barrier, which can make fine lines less obvious. Dehydration makes every line more visible, so drink enough water and avoid excessive alcohol or salty foods right before your big days.

Consider makeup. A light-reflecting concealer (not a heavy, matte one) can diffuse the appearance of creases. Pat it on gently rather than rubbing, and set it with a very fine powder only if needed—too much powder settles into lines and emphasizes them.

## What to do about future injections

If you go back for crow's feet Botox in the future, ask for the original injector if they gave you results you loved. Technique and judgment vary, and [complications with periorbital injectables](https://www.aad.org/public/cosmetic/wrinkles) are more common when the injector is less familiar with your anatomy or less conservative with placement.

Be specific: "I only want crow's feet treated, not under-eye." If someone suggests under-eye Botox again, ask exactly where they plan to inject, what muscle they're targeting, and what the goal is. Under-eye Botox is sometimes used off-label for a very specific concern (like a strong muscle bulge when smiling), but it's not a routine anti-aging treatment. Many experienced injectors avoid it altogether because the risk-benefit ratio isn't great for most people.

If your concern is under-eye hollows or volume loss, [dermal fillers](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/dermal-fillers)—not Botox—are the more common approach, though that comes with its own set of risks and requires an injector with solid anatomical knowledge of the area.

## The takeaway

You're not stuck. The creases you're seeing now are a temporary side effect of the injection, not permanent damage. By your December events, you should be well on your way back to baseline. In the meantime, hydrating patches, good skincare, and strategic makeup can help you feel more like yourself in photos. And next time, stick with what worked—and the injector who delivered it.
