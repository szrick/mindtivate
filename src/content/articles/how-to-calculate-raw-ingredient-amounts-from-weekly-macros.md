## The backward-math problem no one talks about

You've nailed down your daily macro targets—say, 140 g protein, 180 g carbs, 60 g fat. You know what you're cooking. But standing in the grocery store, you're stuck: does a week of that actually require two pounds of chicken breast or three? A bag of rice or two? You don't want to overbuy and watch food rot, but running out by Wednesday derails the whole system.

This isn't a meal-planning problem—it's a yield problem. The protein listed on a nutrition label reflects the cooked, edible portion, but you're buying raw weight. A four-ounce raw chicken breast loses about 25% of its mass to water during cooking, so that same piece weighs around three ounces cooked. Rice does the opposite, roughly tripling in weight when you add water. If you're working backward from your macro goals to a shopping list, you need to account for how much food changes between the store and your plate.

## Why "buying by feel" fails

Most of us learn portion sizes by cooked weight—what 4 oz of grilled chicken looks like on a plate, or a half-cup of cooked quinoa. But grocery stores sell by raw weight, and recipes often specify raw amounts. The USDA's [Food Buying Guide](https://foodbuyingguide.fns.usda.gov/Home/About) was designed to help institutional kitchens translate recipe needs into purchasing quantities, and the same yield logic applies at home: if you don't factor in cooking loss (or gain), you'll consistently over- or underbuy.

That's why gut-feel shopping breaks down. You might remember that one chicken breast "felt right" last week, but if you're prepping five days of lunches and dinners, small errors compound fast.

## The reverse-engineering method

Here's the step-by-step that actually works:

**Start with your weekly macro target.** If you're eating 140 g protein daily and chicken breast is your main source for lunch, decide how much of that daily total comes from chicken—say, 35 g per lunch, so 175 g for a five-day week.

**Look up the cooked nutrition data.** Cooked skinless chicken breast has roughly 31 g protein per 100 g. To get 175 g protein, you need about 565 g of *cooked* chicken (175 ÷ 0.31).

**Apply the yield factor.** Chicken breast has a cook yield of around 75%—meaning 100 g raw becomes roughly 75 g cooked. To end up with 565 g cooked, you need to buy about 755 g raw (565 ÷ 0.75). Round up to 800 g (roughly 1.75 lb) to account for trimming.

The USDA's [Appendix B on yield calculations](https://foodbuyingguide.fns.usda.gov/Appendix/ResourceAppendixB) walks through this same logic with different foods—helpful if you're working with ground meat (higher fat loss), fish (varies by thickness and method), or grains that absorb water.

For carbs, the math flips. If you need 150 g of carbs from rice across the week and cooked white rice is about 28 g carbs per 100 g, you need roughly 535 g cooked rice (150 ÷ 0.28). Dry rice roughly triples when cooked, so you'd buy around 180 g dry—less than half a pound.

## When a spreadsheet beats an app

A simple spreadsheet with columns for food item, target cooked amount, yield factor, and raw amount to buy will get you 90% of the way there. You build it once, plug in your weekly targets, and it spits out a shopping list. The yield factors are widely available (USDA databases, or even the back of the package for some grains).

Apps *can* streamline this—some macro trackers let you log recipes by raw ingredient and auto-calculate cooked nutrition—but many still require you to do the yield math yourself on the front end. If you're already comfortable with your macro targets and just need the purchasing logic, a spreadsheet you control often works better than hunting through an app's database to confirm it's using the same yield assumptions you are.

If you're juggling meal prep with a packed schedule, the [strategy of batching your planning and shopping](/articles/how-to-fit-strength-training-meal-prep-into-60-hour-weeks/) helps too—but the underlying math stays the same.

## One other thing: build in a buffer

Even with precise calculations, real life adds variance. Chicken breasts aren't uniform. You might trim more fat than expected. Someone might eat an extra portion. The [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics recommends](https://www.eatright.org/food/planning/smart-shopping/creating-a-grocery-list) building a small cushion into your shopping list—about 10%—so minor deviations don't derail your week.

That doesn't mean reverting to "buy by feel." It means your 800 g of raw chicken becomes 900 g, and the leftover goes into Thursday's dinner instead of leaving you scrambling Wednesday night.

Once you've done the math for your rotation of staple proteins, carbs, and fats, it becomes automatic. You'll know that your week requires roughly two pounds of chicken, a pound of ground turkey, 200 g of dry rice, and a bag of sweet potatoes—no guesswork, no waste, no mid-week grocery runs.
