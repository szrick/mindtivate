You've ramped up your mileage, added evening gym sessions, and now you're lying in bed wide awake for 30 to 60 minutes despite being physically exhausted. That wired feeling isn't in your head — it's a well-documented response to a sharp increase in training load, and it points to something specific happening in your nervous system.

## Why harder training can keep you wired at night

When you increase your training volume quickly — especially endurance work like marathon prep — your body interprets that as a stressor. Your sympathetic nervous system (the "fight or flight" side) stays more active to keep up with the demand. That's useful during the run or lift, but it doesn't always switch off cleanly when you're done.

[Research on endurance athletes shows that large, rapid increases in training load can impair sleep quality and increase the time it takes to fall asleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11135706/). You're not imagining the change — your body is genuinely struggling to downshift from a revved-up state to a rest-and-digest one.

Evening training sessions make this worse. When you finish a hard workout close to bedtime, your core temperature is still elevated, your heart rate takes longer to return to baseline, and cortisol (a stress hormone that should be dropping in the evening) can stay higher than usual. All of that delays the physiological cues your body needs to feel sleepy.

## The difference between pushing hard and overreaching

Increasing your weekly mileage by 1.5 times over three to four weeks is aggressive. That's not inherently bad — plenty of marathon plans include big jumps — but it does put you closer to what exercise scientists call *functional overreaching*. That's the zone where training stress temporarily outpaces your recovery capacity.

[Sleep disruption is one of the earliest signs that you've tipped into overreaching](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00436/pdf). Other markers include feeling more irritable, a higher resting heart rate in the morning, or workouts that should feel manageable suddenly feeling much harder. If you're seeing several of those alongside the sleep trouble, your body may be asking for a recovery week before you keep building.

## What actually helps when training is disrupting sleep

First, look at the timing of your evening gym sessions. If you're finishing a workout within two to three hours of bedtime, that's likely contributing. Moving those sessions earlier — even by an hour — or swapping one or two for a morning slot can make a noticeable difference.

Second, give your nervous system a clearer wind-down signal. After an evening workout, a cool shower (not ice-cold, just cooler than body temperature) can help bring your core temp down faster. Dim lighting in the hour before bed and keeping your bedroom cool also support that downshift — [your body needs to drop its core temperature to fall asleep efficiently](/articles/does-a-cooler-room-actually-improve-sleep/).

Third, consider whether you're actually recovering enough between hard days. If you're stacking long runs, tempo work, and gym sessions without easier days in between, your body never gets a chance to clear the stress response. A true easy day — where your heart rate stays low and you're not chasing a pace or a rep count — isn't wasted time. It's what allows the hard days to work.

Magnesium is one supplement that comes up often in this context. Magnesium glycinate in particular is better absorbed and less likely to cause digestive upset than other forms. It plays a role in muscle relaxation and nervous-system regulation, and some athletes find it helps with the "wired but tired" feeling after hard training. That said, it's not a substitute for adjusting your training load or workout timing — it's a support tool, not a fix for overreaching.

## When to pull back vs. push through

If the sleep trouble showed up right when you increased your load and you're otherwise feeling okay, adjusting workout timing and adding more intentional recovery may be enough. But if you're also seeing persistent fatigue, mood changes, or performance drops, that's a sign to take a down week — cut your mileage by 30 to 40 percent, keep the intensity low, and let your body catch up.

The joint consensus statement from the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine emphasizes that sleep disturbance is one of the most reliable early warnings that training stress is outpacing recovery. Ignoring it and continuing to ramp up is how functional overreaching turns into overtraining syndrome, which can take months to recover from.

Your body isn't failing you — it's asking for a little more time to adapt. Shifting your evening sessions earlier, building in real recovery days, and giving your nervous system clearer cues to wind down will likely get your sleep back on track without derailing your marathon prep.
