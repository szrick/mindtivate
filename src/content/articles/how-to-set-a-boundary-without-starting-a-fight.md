A lot of people avoid setting boundaries because the few times they
tried, it turned into an argument. That's usually less about the
boundary itself and more about how it got delivered — often as a
reaction, mid-frustration, after the line had already been crossed
several times. Said differently, at a different moment, the same
boundary tends to land as reasonable instead of confrontational.

## Say it before you're at your limit

Boundaries set in the heat of frustration carry all the weight of
everything that led up to that moment, which is exactly what makes the
other person defensive. Naming the boundary early and calmly — before
it's already been broken repeatedly — gives the other person room to
actually hear it instead of just reacting to your tone.

## Lead with the need, not the complaint

"You always interrupt me" invites defensiveness because it's framed as
an accusation about who they are. "I need to finish a thought before
you respond — can we try that?" states the same boundary as a request
about behavior. The second version is genuinely easier to say yes to,
because it doesn't require the other person to agree they've been
doing something wrong.

## You don't need to justify it at length

A boundary that comes with a long explanation can start to sound like
a debate you're inviting the other person to win. "I'm not comfortable
with that" is a complete sentence. You can offer more context if you
want to, but you're not required to build an airtight case just to
have a preference respected.

## Expect some discomfort — that's not the same as it going badly

The first few times you hold a boundary, it can feel tense even when
it's going fine. That discomfort is often just unfamiliarity, both for
you and the other person, not a sign you did something wrong. A
boundary that's genuinely reasonable and calmly stated doesn't need to
be defended forever — it just needs to be repeated, calmly, until it's
the new normal.
