You're exhausted, you've tried everything, and alcohol is the only thing that seems to work — so you're drinking 7-8 drinks most nights just to get six hours of sleep instead of two. It's a brutal choice: rely on something you know isn't great, or lie awake for hours. But here's the hard truth: alcohol isn't actually giving you the restorative sleep your body needs, even when it's knocking you out.

## What alcohol does to your sleep architecture

Alcohol is a sedative, which is why it makes you feel drowsy and helps you fall asleep faster. But sedation isn't the same thing as sleep. [Alcohol disrupts the deeper stages of sleep](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/insomnia/symptoms-causes/syc-20355167) and often causes you to wake up in the middle of the night once your body has metabolized it. That's why people who drink before bed frequently wake at 2 or 3 a.m. and can't get back to sleep.

Your sleep cycles through different stages — light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep — and each one serves a different function. Deep sleep is when your body repairs tissue and strengthens your immune system. REM sleep is when your brain processes emotions and consolidates memories. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night, then causes a rebound effect later that leads to fragmented, lighter sleep and more frequent waking. So even if you're unconscious for six hours, the quality of that sleep is significantly compromised.

## The tolerance trap

The amount of alcohol you need to fall asleep tends to creep up over time. Your brain adapts to the sedative effect, so what worked a month ago stops working now — which is why you're already at 7-8 drinks. That's not a sustainable or safe trajectory, and it puts you at risk for both alcohol dependence and worsening insomnia. When people try to stop using alcohol to sleep after relying on it for a while, their insomnia often gets worse before it gets better, which makes it feel impossible to quit.

## What actually works for chronic insomnia

You mentioned you've tried everything since you were 13, and your doctor didn't help. That's incredibly frustrating, especially when you're desperate for sleep. But there's one intervention that has the strongest evidence for chronic insomnia, and it's not a pill or a supplement: it's called Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia, or CBT-I.

[CBT-I is recommended as a first-line treatment for long-term insomnia](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/treatment) by major medical organizations, and [the American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends it](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) in their clinical practice guidelines. It's a structured, time-limited program — usually six to eight sessions — that addresses the thoughts and behaviors keeping insomnia going. It includes techniques like stimulus control (retraining your brain to associate bed with sleep, not wakefulness), sleep restriction (counterintuitively limiting time in bed to consolidate sleep), and cognitive restructuring (changing the anxious thoughts that make it harder to fall asleep).

CBT-I isn't the same as generic sleep hygiene advice. Sleep hygiene alone — things like keeping your room cool, avoiding screens before bed, skipping caffeine — [isn't recommended as a standalone treatment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/) for chronic insomnia, because it rarely works on its own once insomnia has been going on for years. CBT-I is more targeted and more effective. (If you're curious about one piece of the puzzle, [room temperature does matter physiologically](/articles/does-a-cooler-room-actually-improve-sleep/), but it's not going to fix chronic insomnia by itself.)

## What to do while you're waiting for help

You mentioned saving up for a private appointment. In the meantime, there are CBT-I workbooks and apps (some free, some low-cost) that walk you through the same techniques a therapist would use. They're not a replacement for one-on-one care if your insomnia is severe, but they can give you a structured starting point while you're waiting. The key is following the program consistently for several weeks — it often feels worse before it gets better, which is one reason people abandon it early.

If you're physically dependent on alcohol at this point (meaning you'd experience withdrawal symptoms if you stopped suddenly), don't try to quit cold turkey on your own. Talk to a doctor about tapering safely. [Insomnia and alcohol use disorder often feed each other](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31899990/), and addressing one without the other makes both harder to treat.

## The bottom line

Two hours of sleep is unsustainable, and I understand why you reached for something — anything — that seemed to help. But alcohol isn't giving you the sleep your brain and body actually need, and the amount you're drinking to get even that compromised sleep is putting your health at risk in other ways. Chronic insomnia is miserable, and it deserves real treatment, not just a band-aid that stops working. CBT-I has the strongest evidence base for breaking the cycle, and it's worth pursuing even if it takes time to access.
