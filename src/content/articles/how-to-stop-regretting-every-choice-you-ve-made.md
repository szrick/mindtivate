## Why regret starts to feel like a pattern

You're 28, looking back at your graphic design degree and the string of jobs that didn't stick, and it feels like you've been making the wrong call over and over. That's not unusual—but when regret becomes your default response to past decisions, it stops being about any single choice and starts shaping how you see yourself as a decision-maker.

Psychologist Robert Leahy explains that regret becomes a problem when it shifts from "I made a mistake" to ["I am a mistake"](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/regret)—when you start treating every outcome that didn't go as planned as evidence that you can't trust your own judgment. That's the loop you're in: not just wishing you'd chosen differently, but doubting your ability to choose well at all.

The tricky part? [Regret theory](https://dictionary.apa.org/regret-theory) shows that once you're stuck in that pattern, the fear of future regret starts driving your decisions more than anything else. You avoid committing, you second-guess, or you stay put because at least the status quo feels predictable—even if it's not what you want.

## What makes some regrets stick and others fade

Not all regret works the same way. There's a difference between the kind that teaches you something ("That job wasn't a good fit; now I know I need more autonomy") and the kind that just replays the same self-criticism on a loop ("I should have known better, I always mess this up").

Leahy calls the first kind *productive regret*—it's uncomfortable, but it helps you refine what you're looking for. The second is rumination, and it doesn't actually help you make better choices. It just keeps you focused on what you can't change.

One reason career and education decisions tend to generate so much regret is that they're high-stakes, irreversible (you can't un-take a degree), and the outcomes unfold slowly. You don't know for years whether a choice was "right," so there's plenty of time to second-guess. A [systematic review on decision avoidance and regret](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10575496/) found that people who avoid making decisions—or who preserve the status quo out of fear—often experience *more* regret later, not less. Staying in a job you don't like because you're afraid the next one will also be wrong? That can generate its own regret cycle.

## When chronic regret signals something deeper

If you're regretting *most* of your choices, and that regret is starting to erode your confidence across the board, it's worth asking whether something else is going on. [Research on decision-making and depressive symptoms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3132433/) shows that depression can amplify regret and make it harder to trust your own judgment—you start interpreting neutral or even positive outcomes through a negative lens.

That doesn't mean you're clinically depressed, but if you're also noticing [low energy, trouble enjoying things you used to like, or a general sense that nothing you do will work out](/articles/how-to-restart-a-skincare-routine-after-years-of-nothing/), it's worth talking to a therapist. Chronic regret can be both a symptom and a reinforcing factor.

## How to shift from regret to learning

The goal isn't to never regret anything—that's not realistic, and a little regret can actually be useful. The goal is to stop letting regret define how you see yourself.

**Ask what you actually learned, not what you should have known.** Instead of "I should have picked a different major," try "I know now that I need work that feels more directly useful." That's not the same as saying the degree was a waste—you made friends, you had experiences—but it gives you something concrete to work with going forward.

**Separate the decision from the outcome.** You can make a reasonable choice and still have it not work out. A job that looked good on paper but turned out to have a toxic manager? That's not a failure of judgment; that's new information. You didn't have a crystal ball.

**Notice when you're avoiding a decision because you're afraid of future regret.** If you're staying in a job or a city or a relationship primarily because you're scared the alternative will be worse, that's fear talking—not clarity. Sometimes the right move is to stay, but it should be because you want to, not because you're paralyzed.

**Write down what matters to you now.** Not what you think you *should* want, but what actually feels important. Autonomy? Creativity? Stability? When you're clearer on your priorities, it's easier to evaluate whether a choice aligns with them—and easier to forgive yourself when something doesn't work out, because you can see that you were acting on the best information you had.

## What comes next

You're not broken at decision-making. You've been in a loop where every choice feels like a referendum on your judgment, and that's exhausting. The way out isn't to make perfect choices—it's to stop treating imperfect outcomes as proof that you can't be trusted.

Start small. Make a low-stakes decision this week without second-guessing it afterward. Notice what happens when you let a choice just be a choice, not a test. That's where confidence starts to rebuild—not in getting everything right, but in trusting that you can handle whatever comes next.
