Your daughter is about to start preschool, and you're hoping it'll ease the aggressive, attention-seeking behaviors that showed up after her brother was born. That hope makes sense—more structure, more stimulation, time with other kids. But whether preschool helps depends less on what it takes off your plate and more on what's actually driving the behavior in the first place.

A year after a sibling arrives, aggression in a preschooler usually isn't about needing more to do. It's about needing to know she still matters as much as she did before.

## Why aggression spikes after a new sibling arrives

When a baby joins the family, a toddler or preschooler loses something concrete: your undivided attention. She used to get your full focus during meals, bedtime, play. Now someone else needs you more urgently, more often. From her perspective, the fastest way to get you back is to do something that *makes* you respond—hitting, throwing, yelling. [Attention, even negative attention, reinforces behavior](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/communication/attention.html). If aggression reliably brings you running, it works.

This isn't manipulation in the calculated sense. It's a three-year-old testing whether the relationship still holds, whether she can still pull you toward her when she needs you. The behavior feels wild and exhausting, but it's not random.

## What preschool can (and can't) do

Preschool offers real benefits—[time with other children, routines outside the home, modeling of expected behavior from teachers and peers](https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/preschooler-3-5-years.html). Those things matter for development. She'll learn to share, wait her turn, follow group instructions. She might come home tired enough to make bedtime easier.

But preschool won't directly address the underlying question she's been asking with her behavior: *Am I still important to you?* That question gets answered at home, in the moments when you're not managing a crisis or a meltdown. If the only time she gets your full attention is when she's aggressive, the pattern will likely continue—maybe even intensify when she's tired or overstimulated after a preschool morning.

The good news is that [helping a young child adjust to a new sibling](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/prenatal/Pages/Preparing-Your-Family-for-a-New-Baby.aspx) doesn't require you to split yourself in half or eliminate all conflict. It requires predictable moments when she knows she has you, without having to fight for it.

## What tends to work better than waiting it out

One of the most effective shifts is scheduling short, non-negotiable windows of one-on-one time with her—ten or fifteen minutes where she picks the activity and you're not also holding the baby or checking your phone. It sounds small, but predictability matters more than duration. When she knows that time is coming, the urgency to grab your attention through aggression often decreases. She doesn't have to wonder *if* she'll get you; she knows *when*.

The second piece is how you respond when aggression does happen. [Strategies for handling an aggressive preschooler](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/article/31/11/27/23429/Strategies-for-handling-an-aggressive-preschooler) emphasize calm, immediate consequences paired with teaching the behavior you want instead. "I can't let you hit. If you need me, you can say 'Mommy, I need you.'" Then, critically, you follow through—when she *does* ask without aggression, you respond quickly and warmly, even if it's just to say, "I see you. I'll be there in two minutes."

That's harder than it sounds when you're also keeping a one-year-old alive, but it's the loop that rewires the behavior: asking works better than hitting, and asking gets her what she actually wants.

## Will preschool help? Probably—but not on its own

Starting preschool will likely help in indirect ways. She'll burn energy, build social skills, and have experiences that don't revolve around her brother. You'll have a few hours to regroup, which might leave you with more patience when she's home. Those aren't small things.

But if the question is whether preschool alone will resolve the aggression, the answer is probably not. The behavior is tied to her relationship with you, and that's where the repair happens. Preschool can be part of a larger shift—more structure, more outlets, more support—but it works best alongside the kind of attention that tells her, clearly and repeatedly, that she didn't lose you when her brother arrived.

You've already tried a lot. The fact that you're asking this question means you're paying attention to what she needs, not just managing what she does. That's the part that matters most, and it's the part preschool can't replace.
