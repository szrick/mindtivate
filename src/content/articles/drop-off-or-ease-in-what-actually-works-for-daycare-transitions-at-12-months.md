## The question every parent switching care asks

You've had a nanny at home since your daughter was three months old. She's attached, you work from home, and she sees you throughout the day. Now you're moving her to daycare at 12 months instead of the planned 18, and the gym daycare trials didn't go well. So: do you ease her in with short visits and your presence, or does a clean break—drop her off and leave—actually make it easier?

The short answer is nuanced: a gradual introduction to the *space and caregivers* can genuinely help, but once you're in the daily routine, a quick, consistent goodbye usually works better than lingering. Drawn-out farewells tend to stretch out everyone's distress rather than reduce it.

## Why separation anxiety peaks right now

Around 12 months, [separation anxiety is developmentally normal](/articles/postpartum-rage-the-symptoms-no-one-prepares-you-for/)—not a sign that something's wrong with your child or your parenting. Babies this age have just figured out object permanence (you exist even when they can't see you), but they haven't yet developed a reliable sense that you'll come back. The result is protest: tears, clinging, sometimes outright panic when you leave.

[The American Academy of Pediatrics notes](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Soothing-Your-Childs-Separation-Anxiety.aspx) that this phase typically surfaces between 8 and 14 months and can flare again around 18 months. It's hardest during transitions—new caregivers, new environments, changes in routine—which is exactly what you're navigating.

The fact that your daughter has a "major mama preference" and sees you throughout the day adds another layer—[postpartum transitions affect parents emotionally too](/articles/why-postpartum-rage-feels-worse-with-childless-friends/). She's used to access. Daycare will be the first time she experiences a full stretch without you, and her protest is her way of communicating that she notices.

## What the research says about easing in vs. dropping off

This is where the picture gets more complicated than the typical American advice suggests. [ZERO TO THREE advises against sneaking away](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/separation-anxiety/)—it erodes trust—but also cautions that long, drawn-out goodbyes can signal to your child that there's something to be anxious about.

The [AAP's guidance on child care drop-offs](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/work-and-child-care/Pages/making-baby-drop-off-at-child-care-easier.aspx) recommends developing a short, consistent goodbye routine (a hug, a phrase, a wave), then leaving. Your child will likely cry. The teachers will comfort her. And—this is the part that's hard to believe when you're standing there—many kids calm down within a few minutes of the parent leaving, though this varies considerably by child and age.

But there's another body of research, particularly from European childcare systems, that supports more extended settling-in periods. In Germany, the "Berliner Eingewöhnungsmodell" (Berlin Settling Model) is standard practice: parents stay with their child for the first several days, with gradual separations introduced over one to three weeks. Research on toddler cortisol levels during childcare transitions has found that when parents are present in the initial phase, children show only slight elevations in stress hormones—but in the first period after separation begins, nearly all toddlers show relatively high cortisol levels. One study found that toddlers younger than 14 months showed higher stress levels than older children and may need more time to fully adjust. Importantly, behavioral calm doesn't always reflect internal stress: a child who stops crying may still have elevated cortisol for weeks.

So the evidence supports both approaches to some degree. A gradual introduction to the space—where your child can explore with you as a secure base—can ease the transition. But once she's enrolled and you're doing daily drop-offs, a short, predictable goodbye works better than a prolonged one.

## What makes the transition smoother

A few practical strategies that actually help:

**Predictable routines.** Same wake-up time, same breakfast, same sequence of getting ready, same goodbye phrase. Toddlers rely on predictability when everything else feels uncertain.

**A comfort object.** A small blanket, a stuffed animal, a photo of you—something she can hold that connects her to home. Check with the daycare about their policy, but most allow one small item.

**Consistency across days.** If you stay for 20 minutes on Monday, leave after 5 on Tuesday, and skip Wednesday entirely, she can't build a mental model of what to expect. Pick an approach and stick with it for at least a week or two before deciding it isn't working.

**Keep goodbyes short.** Hug, kiss, "Mama's going to work, I'll be back after snack time," hand her to a teacher, leave. Don't come back for one more hug. Don't linger at the door. It feels brutal, but it works.

**Trust the teachers.** They've seen hundreds of transitions. They know how to comfort a crying 12-month-old, and they'll contact you if she doesn't settle. Many kids stop visibly crying within 5 to 10 minutes of the parent leaving—long before the parent stops crying in the car—though some children, especially those under 14 months, may take longer to truly settle.

## When to worry

Some protest is normal. Inconsolable distress that lasts for weeks, regression in other areas (sleep, eating, language), or a child who seems withdrawn rather than upset can be signs that something isn't working. The Mayo Clinic distinguishes between typical developmental separation anxiety and separation anxiety disorder, which is persistent, excessive, and interferes with daily functioning.

If your daughter is still melting down at drop-off after a month, or if the daycare reports that she's disengaged or distressed for hours rather than minutes, it's worth checking in with your pediatrician. But in the first two weeks? Tears at goodbye are the norm, not the exception.

## The takeaway

Your instinct might be to ease her in slowly, especially because she's used to you being nearby all day. That instinct isn't wrong—a gradual introduction to the new space and caregivers has real value. But once the daily routine begins, the evidence supports a short, consistent goodbye. She'll protest—that's developmentally appropriate—and she'll learn, over days and weeks, that you leave and you come back. That's how she'll learn the pattern that builds trust in this new setting, and it's built through repetition, not through avoiding the discomfort altogether.
