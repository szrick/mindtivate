## Why one month feels fast (and sometimes is)

You went from barely touching your toes to nearly flat in a front split in 30 days. That's genuinely impressive—and also not the norm. Most flexibility timelines cite [six to eight weeks for real tissue-level changes](https://health.mit.edu/sites/default/files/flexibility_stretching.pdf), with lasting structural adaptations taking three to six months. But the early weeks of stretching produce rapid neurological adaptation: your nervous system learns to relax into ranges it already had but was guarding. If you started tight and stretched consistently, that first month can look dramatic.

The catch? Those fast early gains taper. The final few degrees to a completely flat split are the slowest and hardest-won, because you're no longer just teaching your nervous system to chill—you're asking connective tissue to remodel.

## What you're stretching (and why one side is easier)

A front split demands flexibility in two opposing muscle groups: the hamstrings of your front leg and the hip flexors at the front of your back hip. Most people have an asymmetry—one hip is tighter, one hamstring is shorter, or you favor one leg in daily movement. That's why your left side might be nearly there while your right side still feels miles away.

The [kneeling hip-flexor stretch](https://acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/142/kneeling-hip-flexor-stretch/) is a staple for a reason: it isolates the front of the back hip, the part that limits how far you can slide that rear leg backward. Pair it with a lying hamstring stretch or a half-split (hips back, front leg straight), and you're covering both halves of the range a front split requires.

## Why squaring your hips matters more than you think

You mentioned needing to square your hips more, and that instinct is spot-on. When your pelvis rotates—front hip rolling forward, back hip pulling back—you're borrowing range from spinal rotation instead of earning it from your legs. It feels easier in the moment, but it's a compensation pattern that limits real progress and increases the risk of lower-back strain.

Keeping your hips square means both hip bones face forward, your torso stays upright, and the stretch stays where it belongs: in the hip flexors and hamstrings, not your lumbar spine. [Flexibility exercises that emphasize neutral spine and pelvic position](https://acefitness.org/resources/everyone/blog/6499/flexibility-exercises-for-beginners/) help you build the body awareness to catch that rotation before it becomes a habit.

Yoga blocks or a pair of sturdy books under your hands can make a huge difference here. They give you something to press into, which helps you stay tall and square instead of collapsing forward or twisting to one side. You're not cheating by using props—you're giving yourself the support to practice better alignment while your flexibility catches up.

## What happens next (and what doesn't speed up)

The research on stretching modalities shows that [both low-intensity and moderate-intensity stretching improve range of motion over six weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19855344/), but neither study promises a full split in 30 days. Your progress so far reflects consistent effort and probably a decent baseline range you didn't realize you had. Going forward, expect slower, steadier gains.

Stretching to mild discomfort—not sharp pain—is the commonly recommended approach, especially if you're newer to serious flexibility work. That said, the science here is less settled than it's often presented. Some research suggests that stretching at higher intensities (closer to your pain threshold) may actually produce greater range-of-motion gains without necessarily increasing injury risk. The tradeoff between comfort and progress isn't as clear-cut as traditional advice implies. For most people, starting with moderate intensity and gradually exploring your limits makes sense—but "always stay comfortable" isn't a universal rule.

Pushing into sharp or burning sensations does carry risks, though they work through different mechanisms. At the tissue level, aggressive stretching can cause microtears that need time to heal. Separately, your nervous system has its own protective response: push too hard too fast, and it triggers a guarding reflex that tightens the very muscles you're trying to lengthen. One is structural damage; the other is your brain hitting the brakes. Both can set you back, but for different reasons.

Warm up before deep stretches (a few minutes of walking, leg swings, or light movement), and hold each position for 30 to 60 seconds. As for rest days: they matter most for intense, active flexibility work—loaded stretches, end-range isometrics, anything that challenges your muscles under tension. Light passive stretching can generally be done daily without issue. If you're doing aggressive splits training with active engagement, give yourself a day between sessions. If you're just easing into a gentle hip-flexor stretch while watching TV, that's fine to do more often.

## The other side will come—it just needs different attention

Your weaker side isn't broken; it's just tighter. Spend a little more time there—an extra set, a longer hold, or a few minutes of targeted hip-flexor and hamstring work on that leg before you practice the full split. Asymmetry is normal, and evening it out is part of the process, not a sign you're doing something wrong.

If you're curious about [other hip stretches that build foundational mobility](/articles/4-hip-stretches-that-actually-improve-cross-legged-sitting/), the same principles apply: consistency, proper alignment, and patience with the timeline. One month of progress is worth celebrating. The next few months will show you what your body can do when the nervous system and connective tissue both get on board.
