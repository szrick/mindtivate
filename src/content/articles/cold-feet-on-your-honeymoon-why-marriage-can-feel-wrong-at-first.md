## You're not alone—and it doesn't mean you made a mistake

You're on your honeymoon, you've been together twelve years, and suddenly you're questioning everything. That's disorienting. But research on postnuptial depressive symptoms shows that unexpected sadness, disappointment, or emotional complexity right after a wedding is more common than most people admit. The study found that many newly married women experience a letdown period following the wedding—sometimes described as feeling flat, disconnected, or even regretful—even when the relationship itself was solid beforehand.

What you're feeling isn't necessarily about your partner or the twelve years you've spent together. It's about a collision of unmet expectations, unresolved family tension, and the strange emotional aftermath of a high-stakes event that didn't go the way you hoped.

## Why the cake mattered (and why the tears weren't just about cake)

You told your partner the cake was the one thing you were doing for yourself. You have food allergies—finding something safe and exciting to eat isn't trivial. When his sister destroyed it, she didn't just ruin dessert. She disregarded the one boundary you'd clearly named, and your partner presumably didn't intervene in a way that made you feel protected or prioritized.

That's not a small thing. The wedding night ended in tears because the incident crystallized a dynamic you may have been managing quietly for years: his family doesn't respect your needs, and he doesn't always step in. Now that you're married, that pattern feels more permanent. The stakes are higher.

## In-law conflict can affect long-term marital satisfaction—so addressing it now matters

You already strongly dislike his parents. Research published in the *Journal of Marriage and Family* suggests that discord with in-laws—particularly ongoing tension between a wife and her husband's parents—may be associated with declines in marital quality over time. The relationship you have with his family isn't just an annoying side issue. It affects how supported you feel, how much emotional labor you're doing to manage those relationships, and whether your partner consistently has your back when conflict arises.

The honeymoon is supposed to be a reprieve, but you're carrying all of this with you. That makes it hard to feel close to him right now, even if the relationship was good a week ago.

## What "the ick" might be telling you

The ick—that sudden visceral aversion to someone you've loved—is a colloquial term, and researchers are still studying what drives it. Some recent work suggests it may be linked to disgust sensitivity and mate-selection instincts; others frame it as a signal that something in the relationship feels off, even if you can't articulate what. It's not always about attraction, and it's not always about unmet needs—but it's worth paying attention to.

You've been together twelve years. You know him. If this feeling is new or newly intense, it's worth asking: what changed? The answer might be that marriage made implicit things explicit. You're legally family now. The boundaries between your life and his family may feel more porous than they did before. And the disappointments you could previously compartmentalize—his parents' behavior, his sister's thoughtlessness, his response (or lack of response) when you're hurt—suddenly feel like the rest of your life.

That realization can be destabilizing. It doesn't mean the marriage was a mistake. It means you're seeing clearly, maybe for the first time, what needs to change.

## What actually helps when you're in this emotional space

First, give yourself permission to feel what you're feeling without immediately deciding what it means. You don't have to know right now whether this is cold feet, justified anger, or [a sign of a deeper issue](/articles/how-to-stop-regretting-every-choice-you-ve-made/). You're allowed to be upset that your wedding night ended in tears. You're allowed to be furious about the cake. You're allowed to dislike his parents and feel unsupported when they (or his sister) cross a line.

Second, talk to your partner—but not in the middle of the emotional storm. Wait until you're not crying or seething. Then name what happened and why it mattered. "The cake wasn't just cake. It was the one thing I asked for, and when your sister ruined it, I needed you to acknowledge how much that hurt. I didn't feel like you had my back." The [American Psychological Association's guidance on healthy relationships](https://www.apa.org/topics/marriage/healthy-relationships) emphasizes that couples who can communicate openly about unmet needs and repair after conflict tend to fare better long-term than those who avoid difficult conversations.

Third, if the in-law tension is chronic and your partner doesn't see it as a problem, that's the issue to address—not whether you're "overreacting" to the cake. [Research from the Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/about/research/couples/) shows that most relationship conflicts—around 69%—are perpetual, meaning they don't get fully resolved. The goal isn't to [fix every disagreement but to keep talking about the hard stuff](/articles/why-libido-drops-after-kids-and-what-actually-helps/) without getting stuck in gridlock, where one partner feels unheard and the other feels attacked. If that's happening, a few sessions with a couples therapist who specializes in family-of-origin issues can help you both get on the same page before resentment calcifies.

Finally, know that feeling off right after a wedding—especially one that involved family drama and disappointment—doesn't mean you married the wrong person. It means you're human, the event was stressful, and you're processing a lot at once. Give yourself time. But also trust what you're feeling enough to talk about it.
