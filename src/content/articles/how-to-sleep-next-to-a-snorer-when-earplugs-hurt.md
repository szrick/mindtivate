## Why traditional earplugs hurt side sleepers

You've got two weeks to solve a problem that's already cost you a night's sleep: your friend snores, you're sharing a cruise cabin, and standard foam earplugs leave your ears sore by 3 a.m. The issue isn't your pain tolerance—it's the design. Most foam earplugs sit inside the ear canal, and when you press your head into a pillow for hours, that creates sustained pressure on cartilage that wasn't built to bear weight. The plug becomes a lever, and your ear loses.

Side sleepers need something that either stays completely flush with the ear or sits outside the canal entirely. That rules out the high-NRR (noise reduction rating) foam plugs that look best on paper but feel worst in practice.

## Moldable silicone and wax: the low-profile option

Moldable earplugs—silicone putty or wax discs you warm in your hand and press over the ear opening—avoid the canal-pressure problem. They seal the outer ear rather than plugging the canal itself, so there's no rigid cylinder for your pillow to push against. Mack's Pillow Soft silicone is the most commonly recommended version; wax options like Ohropax or Peace & Quiet work similarly but have a shorter reuse window (wax picks up lint and breaks down faster than silicone).

The tradeoff: moldable plugs typically offer 20–24 dB of noise reduction, compared to 30–33 dB for deep-insertion foam. That's enough to take the edge off steady background noise, but snoring—especially the louder, irregular kind—often cuts through. If your friend's snoring is moderate and your white noise machine is already doing some work, moldable plugs may be enough. If it's loud and erratic, you'll probably still hear it.

## Sleep earbuds: noise masking instead of blocking

Sleep-specific earbuds take a different approach. Instead of trying to block all sound, they play white noise, pink noise, or other masking audio to cover the snoring. The newest models (like the Soundcore Sleep A20 and A30, or Ozlo Sleepbuds) are designed with ultra-low-profile housings—some as light as 3 grams per earbud—so they sit nearly flush with your ear when you're on your side.

Many of these also include "snore masking" modes that layer adaptive sound over low-frequency rumbles in real time. [A systematic review of 38 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33007706/) found that continuous broadband noise can help mask disruptive bedroom sounds, though the evidence for sleep *quality* improvement is mixed—some people sleep longer, others just find the environment less annoying. Another [review of 34 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34964434/) involving white and pink noise found no strong overall benefit for sleep, so your mileage will vary. But if your goal is simply to make the snoring less intrusive so you *can* fall asleep, masking audio often works better than passive blocking alone.

The catch: you're wearing electronics in your ears all night. [Cleveland Clinic notes](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/sleeping-with-headphones) that sleeping with earbuds carries some risk—prolonged pressure (even from soft tips), earwax buildup, and a small chance of infection if you're not cleaning them regularly. Keep the volume low, and if your ears feel sore or itchy after a few nights, take a break.

## What to try in the next two weeks

You don't have time to test five products, so here's a realistic plan: pick up a pack of moldable silicone earplugs (Mack's Pillow Soft or a generic equivalent) and try them for a few nights at home with your white noise machine running. If you can still hear your alarm and your ears don't hurt in the morning, that might be enough for the cruise.

If the silicone plugs don't cut it—or if you want a backup plan—consider a pair of sleep earbuds. Most ship quickly, and you'll have time to test the fit and find a masking sound that works for you before you leave. Look for models that specifically advertise a low profile for side sleepers and at least 8–10 hours of battery life (you don't want them dying at 4 a.m.).

One more thing: while you're solving *your* sleep problem, it's worth mentioning that [loud, frequent snoring can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnea](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-to-stop-snoring), which has real health consequences. If your friend doesn't already know they snore this badly, it's worth a gentle heads-up after the trip—not to shame them, but because it's worth checking out.

## The takeaway

Traditional foam earplugs weren't designed for side sleepers, and you're not stuck with sore ears as your only option. Moldable silicone plugs eliminate canal pressure but offer less noise reduction; sleep earbuds add active masking and work better for louder snoring, though they come with their own comfort trade-offs. Test your choice at home before the cruise, keep your white noise machine handy, and know that even an imperfect solution is better than three hours of sleep a night for a week.
