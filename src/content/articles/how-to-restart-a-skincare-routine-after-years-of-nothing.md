## When self-care falls off completely

You're 30, and your skincare routine has been nonexistent for over two years. Depression, a late AuDHD and C-PTSD diagnosis, and the weight of just getting through each day meant that washing your face—let alone following multiple steps—fell off the list entirely. Right now you're using moisturizer after showering, sometimes washing your face with body wash, and that's it. You know you want to do better, but the gap between where you are and where you think you "should" be feels enormous.

Here's the thing: you don't need to leap from zero to a ten-step routine. What you need is a foundation that's simple enough to stick with when executive function is low, effective enough to actually help your skin, and forgiving enough that missing a day doesn't mean you've failed.

## Why routines collapse under mental health strain

When you're managing depression, trauma, or neurodivergence, the invisible load of decision-making and sequencing can make even "simple" tasks feel impossible. [Self-care practices support mental health maintenance](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health), but they're also often the first thing to go when you're struggling—not because you don't care, but because your brain is already running on fumes.

For people with ADHD or autism, the sensory experience of skincare (textures, scents, the feeling of water on your face) and the need to remember and sequence steps can add friction that neurotypical advice doesn't account for. [Mental health challenges and disrupted routines often go hand in hand](https://www.cdc.gov/disability-and-health/articles-documents/mental-health-of-people-with-disabilities.html), especially when you're also navigating disability or neurodivergence. Rebuilding isn't about willpower—it's about designing a routine that works *with* your brain, not against it.

## What a bare-minimum effective routine actually looks like

The good news: dermatologists agree that an effective skincare routine doesn't need to be complicated or expensive. [A basic routine comes down to three steps](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/care/skin-care-budget): a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer, and sunscreen during the day.

That's it. Not seven serums, not a separate eye cream, not acids and retinoids and essences. Those can come later if you want them, but they're not the foundation.

Here's what each step does:

**Cleanser** removes the day's buildup—oil, sweat, environmental grime—without stripping your skin. You want something labeled "gentle" or "hydrating," not "deep-cleaning" or "clarifying," which tend to be harsher. If you're washing your face with body wash right now, switching to a dedicated face cleanser will likely feel gentler (body wash is formulated for thicker skin and can be drying on your face).

**Moisturizer** keeps your skin barrier intact. Even if your skin feels oily, skipping moisturizer can actually make things worse—your skin may overproduce oil to compensate for dehydration. You're already doing this step, so you're halfway there.

**Sunscreen** prevents both premature aging and skin cancer. [Daily sun protection is one of the most effective things you can do for long-term skin health](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/healthier-looking-skin). If the idea of adding another step feels like too much right now, look for a moisturizer with SPF 30 or higher—it's not quite as protective as a separate sunscreen, but it's vastly better than nothing.

## How to make it stick when your brain fights you

The routine itself is simple. Making it happen consistently when you're managing ADHD, depression, or trauma? That takes some strategy.

**Reduce the steps between you and the action.** Keep your cleanser in the shower if that's where you're most likely to use it. Put your moisturizer and sunscreen next to your toothbrush or coffee maker—somewhere you already go every day. The fewer decisions and detours required, the more likely it is to happen.

**Pair it with something you already do.** Habit stacking works well for ADHD brains. If you already moisturize after showering, add cleanser *in* the shower. If you brush your teeth every morning, put sunscreen on right after.

**Give yourself permission to do it "wrong."** Washing your face with just water on a low-energy day is better than not washing it at all. Using moisturizer without cleansing first is fine. Missing a night doesn't erase the previous week. Progress isn't linear, and self-care that only happens when you're functioning well isn't actually sustainable self-care.

**Start with one change.** If you're already moisturizing after showering, maybe the next step is adding a gentle cleanser in the shower three times a week. Not every day, not morning and night—just three times. Once that feels automatic, you can build from there.

## What to look for (and what to skip)

You don't need luxury brands. Drugstore options like CeraVe, Cetaphil, Vanicream, and Neutrogena make gentle, effective products that dermatologists recommend regularly. Look for:

- **Cleanser:** "gentle," "hydrating," or "cream" on the label. Avoid anything that says "foaming" or "for oily skin" unless your skin is genuinely very oily—those tend to be stripping.
- **Moisturizer:** Something that feels comfortable on your skin and doesn't have a scent that bothers you. If fragrance is a migraine trigger or sensory issue, look for "fragrance-free" (not "unscented," which can still contain masking fragrance)—[the same principle applies when choosing body lotion for migraine triggers](/articles/how-to-choose-body-lotion-for-dry-skin-when-fragrance-triggers-migraines/).
- **Sunscreen:** SPF 30 or higher, broad-spectrum. Mineral (zinc oxide or titanium dioxide) sunscreens can leave a white cast but are often gentler for sensitive skin. Chemical sunscreens absorb more invisibly but can occasionally irritate.

Skip anything marketed as "anti-aging," "brightening," or "resurfacing" for now. Those products often contain active ingredients (retinoids, acids, vitamin C) that require more consistency and layering knowledge to use well. You can explore them later if you want to, but they're not part of a foundational routine.

## The goal isn't perfection

Rebuilding a routine after years of nothing—especially while managing mental health and neurodivergence—isn't about getting it right every single day. It's about creating something sustainable enough that it can survive your bad weeks, flexible enough that it doesn't become another source of shame, and simple enough that it actually supports you instead of draining you further.

Three steps. Gentle products. Permission to miss days. That's the foundation. Everything else is optional.
