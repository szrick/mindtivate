## The reality check you need at 6 weeks

Your baby is 6 weeks old and waking every few hours. Meanwhile, you're scrolling posts from parents whose 3-month-olds sleep 8 to 10 hours straight, and you're wondering what routine or method will get you there. Here's the first thing to know: those long stretches aren't the norm at 3 months, and frequent waking at 6 weeks is exactly what's supposed to be happening.

Many babies sleep at least five hours at a time by 3 to 4 months, but an 8- to 10-hour stretch isn't a guaranteed milestone at that age. Some babies get there earlier, some much later—and both are developmentally normal. At 6 weeks, your baby's sleep cycles are still immature, feeding needs are frequent, and nighttime waking is biologically appropriate, not a problem to fix.

## What changes around 3 to 4 months

Babies don't sleep longer stretches because of a specific routine you implement—they do it when their nervous system and circadian rhythm mature enough to support it. [Regular sleep cycles generally develop around 4 months](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/getting-your-baby-to-sleep.aspx), which is when some babies naturally consolidate nighttime sleep.

But here's the catch: that same developmental shift can temporarily make sleep *worse*, not better. Around 4 months, your baby's sleep architecture changes from newborn-style sleep to more adult-like cycles with distinct stages. This transition—often called the 4-month sleep regression—can fragment sleep that had started to consolidate. So if your baby was doing longer stretches at 3 months and suddenly starts waking more at 4 months, that's also normal. It's not backsliding; it's their brain reorganizing how it sleeps.

That maturation is individual. One baby might stretch to six or seven hours at 3 months; another might still wake every three hours at 5 months and be perfectly healthy. Growth, feeding adequacy, and temperament all play a role. The babies sleeping 10 hours at 3 months? They're on the early end of a wide normal range, not the result of a secret method you're missing.

## What a calming nighttime routine actually does

A consistent bedtime routine won't force longer sleep before your baby is ready, but it can [support the transition when their system matures](/articles/you-re-not-ruining-your-baby-by-skipping-drowsy-but-awake/). The goal isn't to train a 6-week-old to sleep through the night—it's to create predictable, calming cues that help your baby wind down.

A simple routine might include a bath, a feed, dim lighting, and quiet settling. Keep nighttime interactions low-key: feed and change your baby without much talking, bright lights, or stimulation. During the day, do the opposite—talk, play, and expose your baby to natural light. That contrast helps reinforce the difference between day and night as circadian rhythms develop.

These routines work with your baby's biology, not against it. You're not teaching a newborn to sleep longer; you're setting up an environment that supports longer sleep when they're developmentally capable.

## The safety piece that matters more than any method

Before chasing longer stretches, make sure the basics are in place. [Always place your baby on their back to sleep on a firm, flat surface](https://safetosleep.nichd.nih.gov/reduce-risk/reduce) with no loose bedding, weighted sleep sacks, or positioning devices. Room-share (but not bed-share) for at least the first six months. These practices reduce SIDS risk and matter more than any sleep-lengthening strategy.

If your baby is gaining weight appropriately and your pediatrician hasn't flagged feeding concerns, you generally don't need to wake a sleeping baby for feeds once they've regained their birth weight and your pediatrician confirms adequate weight gain—usually by 2 to 4 weeks. But that's a conversation to have with your doctor based on your baby's individual growth and health.

## What to do right now at 6 weeks

You don't need to prepare for 8-hour stretches yet. What helps now is managing your own expectations and building sustainable habits for the phase you're actually in.

Respond to your baby's cues. If they're waking to feed every two to three hours, that's normal—not a sign you're doing something wrong. Keep nighttime care calm and boring. Use dim light, minimal interaction, and a predictable sequence so your baby starts to associate nighttime with sleep rather than play.

And if you're touched-out, exhausted, or struggling with the broken sleep, that's also normal. [Postpartum sleep deprivation is real](/articles/postpartum-rage-the-symptoms-no-one-prepares-you-for/), and the anger or frustration that comes with it isn't a character flaw. If you're noticing signs of postpartum mood changes—persistent rage, hopelessness, or intrusive thoughts—talk to your provider. Those symptoms respond to treatment, and you don't have to wait for your baby to sleep longer to get help.

## When longer stretches actually start

Most babies begin sleeping longer stretches somewhere between 3 and 6 months, but the timeline varies widely. Some will surprise you at 10 weeks; others won't consolidate nighttime sleep until closer to a year. That's not as rare as it might sound—research suggests roughly 20 to 30 percent of infants still have fragmented nighttime sleep well past 6 months. If your baby falls into that group, it doesn't mean something's wrong with them or with your approach.

If your baby isn't sleeping longer by 4 or 5 months and you're concerned, talk to your pediatrician. But in the meantime, the most useful thing you can do is build a calm, consistent nighttime routine and trust that longer sleep will come when your baby's system is ready—not because you found the right trick online.
