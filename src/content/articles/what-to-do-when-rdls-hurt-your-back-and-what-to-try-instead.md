You're not alone if RDLs feel like they're working your lower back more than your hamstrings. The Romanian deadlift is one of the most effective posterior-chain exercises out there, but it's also one of the most technically demanding—and when form breaks down, your back takes the hit.

The good news? You don't have to master RDLs to build strong glutes and hamstrings. There are simpler, lower-risk alternatives that let you train the same muscles while you take a break from the frustration.

## Why RDLs are so easy to get wrong

The RDL is a hip-hinge movement, meaning your hips move back and forth while your spine stays neutral. [Research shows that RDLs effectively target the hamstrings and gluteus maximus](https://blog.nasm.org/romanian-deadlift-basics), but they also heavily involve the erector spinae—the muscles that run along your spine. When your hinge pattern isn't quite right, those back muscles end up doing more work than they should, and that's when pain shows up.

A few common form breakdowns increase low-back stress: letting your lower back round or overarch, holding the bar too far from your body, or bending your knees too much and turning the movement into a squat. [ACE Fitness notes that lumbar flexion, extension, or poor bar position can all shift load onto the low back](https://www.acefitness.org/continuing-education/certified/may-2025/8865/the-ace-do-it-better-series-the-romanian-deadlift/) instead of keeping tension in the hamstrings and glutes where it belongs.

If you've been trying for months and still feel it in your back, that's not a sign you're bad at lifting—it's a sign the movement isn't clicking yet, and forcing it isn't going to help.

## Easier alternatives that still train your posterior chain

You don't need to do RDLs to build strong hamstrings and glutes. Here are a few options that offer similar benefits with less technical complexity and lower injury risk.

**Glute bridges and hip thrusts** are excellent for targeting the glutes with minimal low-back involvement. You lie on your back, feet flat, and press your hips up. There's no balance demand, no spinal loading, and the movement is intuitive. You can add a dumbbell or barbell across your hips as you get stronger, or use a resistance band around your thighs to increase glute activation.

**Single-leg Romanian deadlifts with light weight (or bodyweight)** can actually be easier to learn than the barbell version. The lighter load and slower tempo give you more time to feel the hinge pattern, and the single-leg variation forces you to engage your core and glutes for stability. Start with just your bodyweight or a light dumbbell in one hand.

**Resistance band pull-throughs** mimic the hip-hinge pattern of an RDL but with a much more forgiving resistance curve. You anchor a band low behind you, hold it between your legs, and hinge forward and back. The band provides feedback—it pulls you backward, which actually helps reinforce the hinge—and there's no spinal loading. It's a great way to practice the movement pattern without the risk.

**Lying hamstring curls or stability-ball curls** isolate the hamstrings without involving your back at all. They don't train the hip hinge, but they do build hamstring strength, and sometimes that's exactly what you need while you're taking a break from more complex movements.

**Good mornings with a very light load or a resistance band** are another hinge-pattern option. You rest a barbell (or band) across your upper back and hinge forward. The load is lighter and higher on your body than in an RDL, which makes it easier to maintain a neutral spine. Start with just the bar or a band looped around your neck and under your feet.

## When to revisit RDLs (if you want to)

You might decide RDLs aren't worth the effort, and that's completely fine. Plenty of people build strong, functional posterior chains without ever doing them. But if you do want to come back to them later, a few things make the learning curve less steep.

Start with an unloaded hip hinge—no bar, just practicing the movement pattern with your hands on your hips or reaching toward the floor. Once that feels automatic, try it with a very light dumbbell or kettlebell. Only add a barbell once the hinge pattern is solid and you can feel tension in your hamstrings, not your lower back.

Filming yourself from the side helps. You're looking for a neutral spine (no rounding or overarching), hips moving back and forward (not up and down), and the weight staying close to your legs the whole time. If you're working with a coach or trainer, this is one of the movements where in-person feedback makes a real difference.

## The takeaway

RDLs are a great exercise, but they're not mandatory. If they're causing pain or frustration after months of trying, that's your body telling you something isn't working—not that you're doing something wrong as a person. Training your hamstrings and glutes with movements that feel good and let you progress is always going to be more effective than grinding away at an exercise that hurts. Swap in one of the alternatives above, build strength and confidence there, and revisit RDLs only if and when you actually want to.
