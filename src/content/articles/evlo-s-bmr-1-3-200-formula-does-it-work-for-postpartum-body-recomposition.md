## What the Formula Actually Means

EVLO's recommendation—BMR × 1.3 − 200—is a two-step calculation. First, you multiply your basal metabolic rate (the calories your body burns at rest) by 1.3 to estimate your total daily energy expenditure with light activity. Then you subtract 200 calories to create a modest deficit.

For someone with a BMR of 1,400, that's roughly 1,620 calories per day. It's designed to be gentle: a small enough deficit to preserve muscle and energy for workouts, but enough to nudge fat loss over time. Paired with [150 minutes of aerobic exercise per week](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2020/04/physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period)—the same target recommended by ACOG for postpartum women—it's a moderate, sustainable approach.

The question isn't whether the math is sound. It's whether it fits *your* body, your recovery, and your life right now.

## Why Progress Feels Slow Postpartum

Your body in your late thirties, a month postpartum, is not the same body you had at 25. Hormones are recalibrating. Sleep is fractured. Your pelvic floor and core are healing. If you're breastfeeding, your calorie and nutrient needs are higher—and a 200-calorie deficit might actually be too aggressive.

Body recomposition—losing fat while building or maintaining muscle—is *always* slower than straight weight loss. Add postpartum recovery to the mix, and "slow" becomes the norm, not a failure. The fact that you're seeing *any* differences after a month is actually a good sign. Your body is responding; it's just doing so on its own timeline.

## What Actually Drives Body Recomposition

Body recomposition happens when three things align: adequate protein, progressive resistance training, and a calorie intake that supports muscle repair without excessive fat storage.

Protein is non-negotiable. [Research from the International Society of Sports Nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/) suggests that people doing resistance training and aiming for body recomposition benefit from 1.4 to 2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. If EVLO's calorie target leaves you under-eating protein, you'll struggle to build or keep muscle no matter how perfect the workouts are.

Resistance training—like EVLO's low-impact strength work—signals your body to prioritize muscle. But it only works if you're fueling recovery. A 200-calorie deficit is small, but if you're also chasing a toddler, nursing, or chronically under-slept, your real deficit might be much larger. That can stall progress or leave you feeling depleted.

The 150 minutes of cardio is helpful for heart health and creating a bit more calorie wiggle room, but it's not the main driver of recomposition. Strength work is.

## When the Formula Works—and When It Doesn't

This approach works well if:

- You have a reasonably accurate BMR estimate (ideally from a metabolic test, not just an online calculator).
- You're not breastfeeding, or you've adjusted your intake upward to account for it.
- You're getting enough protein—aim for at least 100 grams per day as a starting point.
- You're sleeping enough (or as much as postpartum life allows) and managing stress.
- You're patient. Body recomposition in this phase can take three to six months to show visible change.

It may *not* work if:

- Your actual activity level is higher than "light," making 1.3 too low a multiplier.
- You're undereating protein or total calories and feeling exhausted, losing strength, or seeing your milk supply drop.
- You're comparing your progress to someone who isn't postpartum or who has different hormonal or metabolic factors.

## What to Adjust First

If a month in you're feeling good, recovering well, and seeing small changes, keep going. But if you're dragging, irritable, or stalling, try this: **add 100–150 calories from protein** (a serving of Greek yogurt, an extra egg, a scoop of protein powder) and track your energy and performance for two weeks.

Body recomposition doesn't require perfection. It requires consistency, adequate fuel, and a program that challenges your muscles progressively. EVLO's structure can absolutely work—but the calorie formula is a starting point, not gospel.

One practical note: if you're doing a lot of floor work, planks, or anything that puts pressure on your chest, a high-support sports bra that doesn't dig in—like a wire-free racerback designed for lifting—can make those movements way more comfortable and help you stay focused on form instead of adjusting straps mid-set.

## The Bottom Line

EVLO's formula is reasonable, evidence-aligned, and built for sustainability. But your body's needs postpartum are dynamic. If the scale or measurements aren't moving but you feel stronger, sleep better, and have more energy, that's progress. If you feel depleted or stuck, the fix is usually more food—especially protein—not less. Trust the process, but trust your body more.
