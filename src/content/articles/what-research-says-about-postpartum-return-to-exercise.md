## The 6-week checkup isn't the finish line it feels like

For a lot of women, the postpartum experience goes like this: six weeks after birth, a provider says you're "cleared for exercise," and that gets treated like a green light to pick back up roughly where you left off. The research doesn't actually support that read. Being medically cleared means there's no acute complication stopping you from being active — it isn't a statement that your body has finished adapting to pregnancy and birth, or that your pelvic floor and core are ready for your old training load.

## What general postpartum activity guidance actually recommends

[ACOG's guidance on physical activity during pregnancy and the postpartum period](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32217980/) recommends working back toward about 150 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, in sessions of roughly 20–30 minutes most days — but explicitly frames that as a target to build toward, adjusted for individual recovery, not a day-one prescription. The same guidance notes real benefits tied to returning to activity, including faster postpartum recovery and lower risk of postpartum depression — which is part of why "wait as long as possible" isn't the right instinct either. The actual answer sits between "jump back in at six weeks" and "avoid exercise indefinitely": a gradual, individualized return, not a fixed date.

## Return to running specifically has its own, more cautious timeline

Running gets its own research because it's higher-impact than most other postpartum exercise, and the evidence here is more specific. A [2024 international consensus statement on postpartum return-to-running readiness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38148108/), published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, was developed precisely because existing guidance was vague and runners were left guessing. Its recommendations center on readiness criteria rather than a calendar: an individualized exercise prescription, gradual increases in load, structured walk-run protocols, and targeted strengthening — evaluated against how your body is actually responding, not how many weeks have passed.

## Why pelvic floor and core strength come before impact, not after

The reason running gets treated so differently from walking or swimming comes down to your pelvic floor and abdominal wall. Pregnancy and birth stretch and load both, and [research on postpartum diastasis recti](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11655187/) — the separation of the abdominal muscles down the midline — has found it's linked to pelvic floor dysfunction, since the two systems work together to manage the pressure your core generates with every stride, jump, or heavy lift. Adding impact before that system can handle it is a common driver of leaking, pelvic heaviness, or pain that shows up weeks or months into a return, not on day one. That's the specific reason the running consensus statement above emphasizes targeted muscle strengthening as part of the protocol, not an optional add-on.

## What a realistic, evidence-based progression looks like

Based on this research, a reasonable postpartum return generally starts with walking and basic pelvic floor and deep core activation, moves through low-impact strength work as those systems rebuild capacity, and only adds running or other high-impact training once you can tolerate that load without symptoms — leaking, pelvic pressure or heaviness, or pain — rather than once a certain number of weeks has passed. If any of those symptoms show up as you progress, that's a signal to scale back and rebuild the underlying strength, not to push through it; a pelvic floor physical therapist can assess specifically what your body is and isn't ready for, which a general timeline can't do.

## The takeaway

"Cleared at six weeks" is a real milestone, but it answers a narrower question than most people assume — whether anything is medically stopping you, not whether your pelvic floor and core are ready for your previous training load. The actual evidence points toward a gradual, symptom-guided return: general activity first, targeted pelvic floor and core strengthening before impact, and running (or anything similarly high-impact) added back based on how your body responds, not a date on the calendar.
