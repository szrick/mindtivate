## Why you can't sit cross-legged comfortably

If your legs won't cross lower than mid-shin without strain, you're dealing with limited hip mobility—specifically, restricted external rotation and abduction (the movement that lets your thigh rotate outward and move away from your body's midline). This isn't about flexibility alone. Your hip is a ball-and-socket joint, and comfortable cross-legged sitting requires the ball (your femur head) to rotate smoothly in the socket while surrounding muscles—hip flexors, adductors, glutes, and external rotators—allow the movement without pulling back.

Tight hip flexors from prolonged sitting, short adductors (inner thigh muscles), or weak external rotators all limit how far your knee can drop toward the floor. The Mayo Clinic notes that regular stretching improves range of motion in joints, and for hips specifically, that means targeting multiple muscle groups—not just one.

## The four stretches that address the real restrictions

**Butterfly stretch (for adductors and external rotation)**  
Sit on the floor, bring the soles of your feet together, and let your knees fall out to the sides. Hold your feet and gently press your knees toward the floor with your elbows—don't force it. You should feel a stretch along your inner thighs. Hold for 30 seconds, relax, repeat. If your knees are high off the ground, that's fine—it'll improve with consistency.

**90/90 hip stretch (for external and internal rotation)**  
Sit with one leg bent in front of you at 90 degrees (knee out to the side, shin roughly perpendicular to your torso) and the other bent behind you, also at 90 degrees. Both hips should stay on the floor. Lean forward slightly over your front leg. This stretch hits both the external rotators of your front hip and the internal rotators of your back hip. Hold 30–45 seconds each side. If one hip lifts off the floor, sit on a folded blanket or yoga block under that side.

**Low lunge (for hip flexors)**  
Kneel on one knee, step the other foot forward so your knee is over your ankle, and shift your hips forward until you feel a stretch in the front of your back hip. Keep your torso upright—don't lean forward. Tight hip flexors pull your pelvis forward, which restricts how much your thigh can rotate outward. The [American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons' hip conditioning program](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/globalassets/pdfs/hip-conditioning-rehab-program-2025.pdf) includes hip flexor stretches as part of rehab protocols for improving hip range of motion. Hold 30 seconds each side.

**Pigeon pose (for glutes and deep external rotators)**  
From hands and knees, bring your front knee toward your same-side wrist, with your shin angled across your body—the closer your shin is to parallel with the top of your mat, the more intense the stretch, so start with your foot closer to your opposite hip if needed. Extend the other leg straight behind you and lower your hips toward the floor. You'll feel this in the glute and outer hip of your front leg. If your hips don't reach the floor, place a folded blanket or block under the front hip for support. Hold 45–60 seconds each side.

## How often to stretch (and why it matters)

You won't see change from stretching once a week. The Mayo Clinic recommends stretching major muscle groups at least two to three times per week for general flexibility gains. If you're starting from significant restriction and want to accelerate progress, daily or near-daily stretching may help—but three to four days a week is enough to see real improvement over time. Consistency matters more than duration: 10 minutes of focused hip stretching regularly will get you further than an hour-long session once a week.

Warm up first. Stretching cold muscles increases injury risk and limits how far you can safely go. A few minutes of walking, marching in place, or leg swings is enough.

If you're using props—a yoga block to sit on during the 90/90, a strap to hold your feet in butterfly—that's not cheating. Props let you hold a stretch in better alignment, which means you're actually working the right tissues instead of compensating with your lower back or [forcing a position your body isn't ready for yet](/articles/should-you-use-sliders-for-stretching-and-flexibility/).

## What to expect (and when to ease up)

You should feel a stretch—a pulling sensation in the muscle—but not pain. Sharp pain, pinching in the front of your hip, or pain that doesn't ease when you back off means you're pushing too hard or there's something structural going on that stretching alone won't fix.

Progress isn't linear. Some days your hips will feel more open; other days, tighter. That's normal. If you've been sitting all day, your hips will be stiffer than after a walk. Over weeks, though, you should notice your knees dropping lower in butterfly, your hips sinking closer to the floor in pigeon, and cross-legged sitting feeling less effortful.

If after six to eight weeks of consistent stretching you're not seeing any change—or if you have pain during or after stretching—talk to a physical therapist. Some hip restrictions come from bony anatomy (how your femur sits in the socket), previous injury, or labral issues that won't respond to stretching alone and need a different approach.
