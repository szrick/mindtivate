## The pattern that signals more than just awkwardness

Your boyfriend stays close to his ex-girlfriend's three-year-old daughter — he's the godfather, he cares about her, and that's not inherently a problem. But he'll drive 12 hours to see that child while refusing to let your own kids come to his house. That asymmetry isn't about his role as godfather. It's about how he's choosing to show up (or not show up) for the family you're trying to build together.

The issue isn't that he maintains contact with a child from a past relationship. The issue is the double standard, and what it reveals about how he sees your children — and by extension, you — fitting into his life.

## Why the "no feelings for my ex" reassurance misses the point

When you raise concerns and he responds by insisting he has no romantic feelings for his ex, he's answering a question you didn't ask. You're not worried he's going to rekindle things with her. You're worried that he's willing to make significant effort and emotional investment for her child while actively excluding yours.

Healthy relationships require [trust, respect, and clear communication](https://wellness.uchicago.edu/healthy-living/health-information/healthy-relationships/) about needs and expectations. When one partner deflects a boundary concern by reframing it as jealousy or insecurity, that's a communication breakdown — and often a sign that the real issue is being avoided.

The question isn't whether he still loves his ex. It's whether he's genuinely ready and willing to integrate your children into his life, or if he sees them as optional while treating his ex's child as a priority.

## What a boundary conversation actually needs to address

If you haven't yet named the specific behavior that doesn't work for you, that's where this conversation needs to start. [Setting a boundary](https://extension.usu.edu/relationships/faq/how_to_set_healthy_bounderies) means stating clearly what you need, not just expressing discomfort in general terms.

For example: "I need to understand why my children aren't welcome at your house when you're willing to travel 12 hours for your ex's daughter. If we're building a future together, I need to know that my kids are part of that picture, not an afterthought."

That's different from asking him to stop seeing his goddaughter. You're asking him to explain the discrepancy and to make space for your children in a way that reflects the seriousness of your relationship. [How you frame that request](https://www.apa.org/topics/healthy-relationships) — and how he responds — will tell you a lot about whether this is a fixable miscommunication or a fundamental mismatch in priorities.

## When it's a compatibility issue, not just a boundary issue

Blended families are complicated, and managing relationships with exes when children are involved requires patience, flexibility, and a lot of ongoing negotiation. But [blended-family conflict](https://www.gottman.com/blog/managing-conflict-in-blended-families/?e-page-e7b509d=2) becomes unmanageable when one partner isn't willing to do the work of integration — when they want to keep their old commitments intact while refusing to make room for new ones.

If he's unwilling to have your children in his home, that's not a boundary. That's a choice about what kind of relationship he's willing to have with you. A partner who sees a future with you would be working toward a life that includes your kids, even if that process is gradual and sometimes awkward.

The fact that he's making a 12-hour trip for his goddaughter while keeping your children at arm's length suggests he's comfortable with the relationship staying compartmentalized. That might work for him, but it clearly doesn't work for you — and it's worth asking whether it ever will.

## What to do when the conversation doesn't shift anything

If you've named the problem clearly and he still doesn't see why the double standard is an issue, you're not dealing with a communication problem anymore. You're dealing with a values problem.

Some people are genuinely ready to blend families and make space for a partner's children. Others want the relationship but not the full integration. Neither is wrong, but they're not compatible with each other.

You don't need his permission to decide this isn't working. If he's shown you through his actions — not just his words — that your children aren't a priority, you're allowed to believe him. Waiting for him to change his mind or hoping he'll eventually come around often just delays a decision you already know you need to make.

Your kids are watching how you let yourself be treated, and they're learning what's acceptable in a relationship. That's not meant to guilt you — it's just true. If this relationship requires you to accept that your children matter less than his ex's child, that's not a compromise. That's a cost, and it's worth asking yourself whether you're willing to keep paying it.
