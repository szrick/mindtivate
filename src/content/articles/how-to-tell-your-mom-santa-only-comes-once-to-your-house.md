## Why this conversation feels so hard

You're not even dealing with the issue yet—your baby won't remember this Christmas—but you already know the conversation is coming. Your mom has started talking about Christmas Eve traditions, the ones you grew up with, and you can hear in her voice how much she wants to recreate them with your child. Meanwhile, you and your husband have your own vision: Christmas morning, Santa at your house, your own family's start.

The guilt hits before you've even said anything. She's excited. She loves you. She wants to share something meaningful. And you're about to tell her no.

But here's the thing: wanting to establish your own family's traditions isn't selfish or hurtful. It's part of becoming a parent. The hard part is that setting a boundary doesn't mean you're responsible for how someone else feels about it—even when that someone is your mom, and even when her feelings are disappointment.

## What you're actually asking for

Before you have the conversation, it helps to get clear on what you want. You're not asking your mom to skip Christmas or to stop being involved. You're saying that Santa—the magic, the surprise, the morning reveal—happens at your house. That's it. 

That leaves plenty of room for her: Christmas Eve dinner, a different gift-giving tradition (maybe she's the one who gives the special pajamas, or a single ornament, or the first book of the year), FaceTime on Christmas morning, or a separate celebration on another day. She can still be deeply involved. She just won't be the house where Santa comes.

Getting specific about what you *are* offering makes the conversation feel less like a shutdown and more like a redirect.

## How to say it

Timing matters. Don't wait until December 23rd when she's already bought gifts and planned the evening. Have the conversation now, while it's still abstract, and frame it as something you've been thinking about as you plan your family's future.

Using "I" statements and being direct makes this easier. Try something like: "Mom, I've been thinking about Christmas traditions, and I wanted to talk to you before things get too set. We've decided that we want to do Santa at our house on Christmas morning. I know that's different from how we did it growing up, and I don't want you to feel like we're shutting you out—we absolutely want you to be part of the holidays. But this is something that feels important to us as we start our own family."

Then pause. Let her respond. She might be disappointed, and that's okay. You don't have to fix it or talk her out of her feelings. Acknowledge them: "I know this isn't what you were picturing, and I get that it's hard." But don't apologize for the boundary itself.

If she pushes back—"But you loved it when we did it this way" or "The baby won't even know the difference"—you can stay calm and repeat the same idea in slightly different words. "I did love it, and I'm so glad that's a memory I have. But we want to create our own version now." Or: "You're right that the baby won't know this year, but we're setting this up for the long term, and it's easier to start now than to change it later."

## What to offer instead

Once you've stated the boundary, pivot to what *is* available. "We'd love for you to come over Christmas morning after we do stockings," or "Would you want to host Christmas Eve dinner? We could do a special tradition that's just yours—maybe you're the one who gives the Christmas book every year, or we always make cookies at your house the weekend before."

[Maintaining a close relationship with grandparents](https://extension.usu.edu/relationships/faq/relationship-with-grandparents) doesn't require giving them every tradition. It requires genuine connection, shared time, and respect on both sides. If your mom feels like she has her own meaningful role, the loss of the Santa tradition will sting less.

And if she's still hurt? That's not a sign you did something wrong. It's a sign she's adjusting to a shift in her role, which is a real loss for her—but not one you need to prevent by giving up what matters to you.

## When it doesn't go smoothly

Some parents will hear this once and adjust. Others will keep bringing it up, testing the boundary, or making comments designed to make you feel guilty. If that happens, you'll need to restate it, calmly and without anger: "We've already talked about this, and the plan isn't going to change. I'd love to move on and talk about what we *can* do together."

If she refuses to let it go, you might need to limit the conversation: "Mom, I'm not discussing this again. Let's talk about something else." Then actually change the subject or end the call if she won't.

It feels harsh when you write it out like that. But repeatedly relitigating a decision you've already made doesn't protect the relationship—it just teaches her that if she pushes hard enough, you'll cave. Holding the boundary, even when it's uncomfortable, is what lets you build a relationship where both of you can be honest.

## The long view

Your baby is ten months old. You have years of holidays ahead, and this first conversation is setting the tone for all of them. If you can establish now that you and your husband make the decisions about your family's traditions—and that your mom's role is still valuable, just different—you'll have an easier time when the next issue comes up. Because it will. [Bedtimes, screen time, food rules, school choices](/articles/postpartum-rage-the-symptoms-no-one-prepares-you-for/)—there's always something.

The parents who regret setting boundaries are vanishingly rare. The ones who regret *not* setting them early? That's a longer list.
