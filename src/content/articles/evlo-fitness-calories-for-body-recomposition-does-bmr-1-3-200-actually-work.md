## What EVLO Is Asking You to Do

Let's break down the formula: BMR × 1.3 − 200. Your basal metabolic rate (BMR) is what your body burns at complete rest—just keeping your heart beating, lungs breathing, and cells functioning. Multiplying by 1.3 adds a modest activity factor, then subtracting 200 calories creates a small deficit. Pair that with 150 minutes of aerobic exercise weekly, and the idea is you'll lose fat while building muscle through low-impact strength work.

On paper, it's a reasonable middle-ground approach. The 1.3 multiplier assumes light daily activity, and a 200-calorie deficit is gentle enough that it shouldn't tank your energy or recovery. The [150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity aligns with postpartum exercise guidelines](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/exercise-after-pregnancy) and supports cardiovascular health without overdoing it.

## Why Progress Feels Slow Postpartum

If you're a month in and only seeing subtle changes, that's completely normal—and actually a good sign. Your body is still in a significant recovery phase in your late thirties postpartum. Hormones are recalibrating, sleep is likely disrupted, and your tissues are healing. Expecting rapid transformation in this context isn't realistic, and frankly, a program that promises that would be a red flag.

Body recomposition—losing fat while maintaining or building muscle—is inherently slower than straight weight loss. You're asking your body to do two metabolically demanding things at once. The scale might barely budge, but measurements, how your clothes fit, and strength gains are better markers. If you're noticing differences in how you feel and move after just four weeks, you're on track.

## The Calorie Formula: What It Gets Right (and What It Misses)

The EVLO formula's strength is its conservatism. A 200-calorie deficit is small enough that most women won't feel deprived or see performance drop-offs. It also leaves room for adjustment based on hunger, energy, and whether you're breastfeeding—[postpartum energy needs vary significantly depending on lactation status and activity level](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26818/page/154).

But here's the catch: BMR calculators are estimates, and the 1.3 activity multiplier might not fit your actual life. If you're chasing a toddler, doing EVLO workouts, and hitting 150 minutes of cardio, your real activity factor could be closer to 1.5 or higher. Undereating by too much—especially when you're strength training—can stall progress because your body won't have the resources to repair and build muscle.

The formula also doesn't directly address protein, which is critical for body recomposition. [Research shows that higher protein intake combined with resistance training supports lean mass retention and fat loss](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/), particularly in a deficit. If you're following the calorie target but skimping on protein, you might lose weight without the muscle-building payoff you're hoping for.

## What Actually Matters for Body Recomposition

Calories are one piece, but they're not the whole story. Here's what tends to move the needle:

**Consistency over perfection.** Sticking with a program for months, not weeks, is what creates visible change. The fact that you love EVLO a month in is huge—enjoyment predicts adherence better than any formula.

**Adequate protein.** Aim for roughly 0.7–1 gram per pound of goal body weight, spread across meals. This gives your muscles the building blocks they need, especially in a deficit.

**Progressive overload.** Even with low-impact work, you need to gradually increase challenge—more reps, slower tempos, resistance, or range of motion. Muscle growth requires a reason to adapt.

**Real recovery.** Sleep, stress management, and rest days aren't optional. Your body recomposes during recovery, not during the workout itself.

**Patience with your postpartum timeline.** Hormonal shifts, especially if you're breastfeeding or recently weaned, affect how your body responds to training and nutrition. What worked in your twenties might need tweaking now.

## Should You Stick with It?

If you're seeing differences, feeling strong, and enjoying the program, keep going. The formula is a starting point, not gospel. Pay attention to your hunger, energy, workout performance, and how your body is changing over the next two to three months. If progress stalls or you feel run-down, consider adding 100–200 calories back—especially from protein or carbs around your workouts—rather than cutting further.

Body recomposition postpartum isn't about finding the perfect calorie number. It's about building sustainable habits, fueling your training, and giving your body the time and resources it needs to change. You're one month in and already noticing shifts. That's not slow—that's exactly how this works.
