When you have severely dry skin or ichthyosis, lotion isn't optional—it's a daily necessity. Going through multiple bottles a week adds up fast, and the math gets frustrating when you're comparing per-ounce pricing across drugstore aisles. The question isn't whether you need thick, effective moisturizer. It's how to get enough of it without the cost spiraling.

## Why thick formulas matter for very dry skin

If you've found that thin lotions don't cut it, there's a physiological reason. [Creams are thicker and more hydrating than lotions](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/pick-moisturizer) because they contain more oil and less water—dermatologists specifically recommend creams or ointments rather than lotions for very dry or compromised skin. That thicker consistency creates a better barrier to lock in moisture, which is exactly what conditions like ichthyosis need. While [drinking more water has limited direct impact on skin appearance](/articles/does-drinking-more-water-actually-improve-skin/), topical hydration through the right moisturizer makes a measurable difference for compromised skin barriers. A watery lotion may feel lighter going on, but it evaporates faster and doesn't seal hydration the way a heavier product does.

The trade-off is that thicker products tend to cost more per ounce in smaller containers, and when you're applying multiple times a day to your whole body, a standard 8- or 16-ounce bottle disappears quickly.

## The cost-per-ounce calculation that actually matters

You've already done the math on drugstore staples—Nivea in the blue jar at $0.73/oz, Aveeno at $0.55/oz, CeraVe at $0.99/oz. Those are solid benchmarks. The next step is looking at larger formats: tubs in the 16–20 oz range, or institutional/bulk sizes that come in pump dispensers or gallon containers.

Some options to compare:

- **CeraVe Moisturizing Cream** comes in a 19 oz tub (often around $16–18, bringing it closer to $0.85–0.95/oz) and a less common 1 lb jar.
- **Eucerin Original Healing Cream** is available in 16 oz jars, often around $12–14 ($0.75–0.87/oz).
- **Vanicream Moisturizing Cream** comes in a 1 lb (16 oz) tub, frequently under $13 ($0.81/oz), and is fragrance-free and dye-free—relevant if you're trying to avoid irritants.
- **Aquaphor Healing Ointment** (more occlusive than a cream, so greasier) comes in a 14 oz jar, usually $13–15 ($0.93–1.07/oz).

For true bulk, some people turn to suppliers that serve healthcare facilities or salons. Brands like **Medline Remedy** or **McKesson** offer unscented body creams in gallon pump bottles, but availability varies and per-ounce pricing can be better or worse depending on whether you're buying direct or through a reseller.

## When to add oil instead of buying a richer base

You mentioned keeping jojoba oil on hand to adjust thickness. That's a smart move—buying a moderately thick cream in bulk and customizing it with a few drops of oil (jojoba, squalane, or even plain mineral oil) can be more economical than buying the richest formulation outright. [Dermatologists recommend applying moisturizer within a few minutes after bathing](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/dermatologists-tips-relieve-dry-skin) while skin is still damp, which is also when adding a layer of oil on top works best to seal everything in.

One caution: if you're mixing oil into the product itself (rather than layering), do it in small batches. Adding oil to a large container can dilute preservatives and shorten shelf life, especially if you're introducing any moisture or bacteria from hands or tools.

## Fragrance-free vs. lightly scented: what the research says

You said you can tolerate scents but prefer unscented. For very dry or sensitive skin, [fragrance-free products are consistently recommended](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000751.htm) because fragrance—even "natural" essential oils—is one of the most common irritants. If a product works for you and happens to have a light scent, that's fine. But if you're comparing two equally thick, equally affordable options, the unscented version is the safer bet, especially if you're applying multiple times a day over large areas of skin.

## What "lotion clothing" says about the real cost

The fact that you have dedicated sets of clothing you don't mind getting greasy speaks to how much product you're actually using—and how much the cost adds up. That level of use is exactly why per-ounce pricing and bulk formats matter. A few practical strategies:

- **Buy during sales and stack coupons.** Drugstore chains often run buy-one-get-one or percentage-off deals on larger tubs. Stock up then.
- **Check warehouse clubs.** Costco and Sam's Club sometimes carry multi-packs of CeraVe, Eucerin, or Cetaphil at lower per-ounce pricing than drugstores.
- **Look at subscription discounts.** Amazon Subscribe & Save or Target's subscription service can knock 5–15% off repeat orders, which adds up over time.
- **Consider store brands.** Many drugstore chains make their own versions of CeraVe or Eucerin formulas (often labeled "Compare to...") at 20–30% lower prices. Ingredient lists are usually very similar.

## The bottom line

When you need thick, effective moisturizer in large quantities, the goal is finding the largest size of a formula that actually works for your skin, then buying it as efficiently as possible. That might mean a 19 oz tub of CeraVe on sale, a gallon pump of institutional cream if you can source it reliably, or a moderately priced thick cream you boost with your own oil. The per-ounce math matters, but so does the reality that a cheaper product you have to reapply constantly isn't actually saving money.
