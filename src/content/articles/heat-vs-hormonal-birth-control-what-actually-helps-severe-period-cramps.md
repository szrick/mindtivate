## When painkillers don't cut it

You're three days into your period. The cramping is so intense you can barely move, and the ibuprofen you took an hour ago has done almost nothing. You've heard that birth control can help with severe cramps—but you've also heard horror stories about IUD insertion pain, and you're not sure if pills are any better. Meanwhile, your mom keeps suggesting a heating pad, which sounds too simple to actually work.

Here's the thing: both heat and hormonal contraception have solid evidence behind them for menstrual pain relief, but they work in completely different ways. One addresses the symptom (the cramping itself), and the other can reduce the underlying cause (the hormonal cascade that triggers heavy, painful periods in the first place).

## Why cramps get so severe in the first place

Severe menstrual cramps—clinically called dysmenorrhea—happen when your uterus releases high levels of prostaglandins, hormone-like substances that trigger strong uterine contractions to shed the lining. More prostaglandins mean more intense contractions, which reduce blood flow to the uterus and cause that deep, gripping pain. [Period pain typically lasts up to three days](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/period-pain/), which matches exactly what you're experiencing.

A heavy flow often goes hand in hand with severe cramps because both are driven by the same prostaglandin surge. When over-the-counter NSAIDs like ibuprofen don't provide enough relief, it's a sign that your pain is either particularly severe or that the medication isn't reaching therapeutic levels—but it's also a signal that you need more support, not just to push through it.

## What heat actually does for cramps

Applying heat to your lower abdomen or lower back isn't just comfort—it's a physiological intervention. Heat increases blood flow to the pelvic area, which may help dissipate prostaglandins and relieve the ischemia (reduced blood flow) causing pain. It also relaxes the uterine muscles directly, reducing the intensity of those painful contractions.

Research backs this up. A 2025 systematic review found that heat therapy showed a 45% relative difference in pain reduction within 24 hours compared to no treatment, with efficacy comparable to NSAIDs—and about 70% lower risk of side effects. Heat won't prevent cramps from happening in future cycles, but it's a safe, accessible tool for managing acute pain when it hits.

You can use a traditional plug-in heating pad, a microwaveable heat pack, a hot water bottle, or even a warm bath. The key is consistent application during those first brutal days—keeping it on your lower abdomen or back for several hours at a time, not just a quick 15-minute session.

## How hormonal birth control reduces period pain

Hormonal contraception works differently. Instead of treating the pain after it starts, it reduces the hormonal fluctuations that cause heavy, painful periods in the first place. [The combined pill and hormonal intrauterine system (IUS) can both reduce period pain](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/periods/period-problems/) by thinning the uterine lining, which means less tissue to shed, fewer prostaglandins released, and lighter, less painful periods overall.

The hormonal IUS (like Mirena or Kyleena) releases progestin locally in the uterus. For many people, periods become much lighter or stop altogether within a few months. The combined birth control pill works similarly but through a systemic hormone dose that prevents ovulation and keeps the uterine lining thin.

Both options take time to show their full effect—usually a few months, and you may experience [breakthrough bleeding during the adjustment period](/articles/abnormal-bleeding-on-birth-control-when-to-worry-about-pregnancy/). And yes, IUD insertion can be painful (though experiences vary widely and pain management options exist). The pill avoids that insertion issue but requires daily adherence. If you're leaning toward birth control primarily for period pain rather than contraception, it's worth discussing both options with your provider to weigh the trade-offs. You can read more about [how birth control affects mood](/articles/how-birth-control-can-affect-your-mood/) if that's a concern for you.

## When to see a doctor about severe cramps

If your cramps are regularly severe enough that over-the-counter painkillers barely help, that's worth medical attention—not just to get a prescription for birth control, but to rule out underlying conditions like endometriosis, fibroids, or adenomyosis. [The NHS advises urgent assessment when pain is severe or worse than usual and painkillers have not helped](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/period-pain/).

A healthcare provider can also discuss stronger pain relief options, whether that's prescription-strength NSAIDs, a specific type of hormonal contraception tailored to your needs, or other interventions if an underlying condition is found.

## What to do right now

You don't have to choose between heat and birth control—they're not mutually exclusive. While you're figuring out whether hormonal contraception makes sense for you (and waiting for an appointment), heat therapy is something you can start using today. It's low-risk, inexpensive, and evidence-based.

If you decide to try birth control for period pain, keep using heat during your cycles in the meantime, and for the first few months after starting hormones while your body adjusts. And if your cramps are this severe, don't wait too long to talk to a doctor. Pain that interferes with your life for three days every month isn't something you just have to live with.
