## When waking too early becomes a real problem

You've figured out that you feel your best on 8.5–9 hours of sleep, but most weeks you're waking too early and landing under 7.5 hours instead. You're exhausted, it's affecting your life, and you're wondering whether this actually counts as insomnia—especially since 7 hours is technically "normal" for adults.

Here's the thing: insomnia isn't defined by hitting some universal sleep-hour threshold. It's about whether you're getting *enough* sleep for *you*, and whether the lack of it is causing problems during the day. If you're regularly waking too early, can't get back to sleep, and you're dragging through your days as a result, that's worth taking seriously.

## What insomnia actually means

[Insomnia](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia) is trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or getting good-quality sleep even when you have the opportunity to rest. The "staying asleep" part—waking during the night or too early in the morning—is called sleep maintenance insomnia, and it's just as legitimate as the can't-fall-asleep kind.

The other piece is daytime impairment. [Symptoms include](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/symptoms) feeling tired during the day, mood changes, difficulty concentrating, and generally not functioning the way you know you can. You're describing exactly that: you *know* you need more sleep because you've felt the difference, and right now you're not getting it most days of the week.

Chronic insomnia is diagnosed when this pattern happens at least three nights a week for three months or longer. If you're hitting 4–5 days a week of insufficient sleep and it's been going on for a while, you're well within that range.

## Why "normal" sleep ranges don't tell the whole story

The often-cited 7–9 hour range for adults is a population average, not a one-size-fits-all prescription. Some people genuinely do fine on 7 hours. Others need closer to 9. You've already done the observational work here—you feel your best around 8.5–9 hours, and you feel terrible on less than 7.5. That's your baseline, and it matters more than the general guideline.

The fact that you sometimes struggle to fall asleep *and* stay asleep suggests your sleep system is dysregulated in more than one way. It's not uncommon for both to show up together, especially when stress, anxiety, or an overactive nervous system is involved.

## What's worth trying (and when to talk to someone)

If this is a newer pattern—say, a few months rather than years—start with the basics: consistent wake time (even on weekends), limiting caffeine after early afternoon, keeping your bedroom cool and dark, and winding down without screens for the last hour before bed. Some people find a small protein-and-fat snack before bed (a spoonful of nut butter, a few bites of cheese) helps them stay asleep longer, though the evidence for this in people without blood sugar conditions is mostly anecdotal.

Magnesium is one supplement that comes up often for sleep maintenance issues. Magnesium glycinate in particular is the form most commonly recommended for sleep, since it's well-absorbed and less likely to cause digestive upset than other types. That said, research on magnesium and sleep shows modest effects at best—it may help some people, but it's not a reliable fix. If the underlying issue is anxiety, a racing mind, or something else physiological, magnesium alone is unlikely to solve it.

If you've been dealing with this for months, it's consistently affecting your day-to-day life, and basic sleep hygiene tweaks aren't moving the needle, it's worth talking to a healthcare provider. The [CDC recommends](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html) seeing someone if you regularly have trouble sleeping, and what you're describing—frequent early waking, chronic exhaustion, a clear gap between the sleep you need and the sleep you're getting—fits that bill. A provider can help rule out other causes (sleep apnea, restless legs, hormonal shifts, depression) and discuss options like cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which has strong evidence for treating chronic sleep maintenance problems.

## The takeaway

You're not imagining this, and you're not being high-maintenance for needing more than 7 hours. Waking too early most days of the week and feeling exhausted as a result meets the general criteria for insomnia—and more importantly, it's affecting your life. That means it's worth addressing, whether through habit changes, targeted supplements, or working with someone who can dig into what's driving the pattern and rule out other conditions.
