## The real problem isn't motivation—it's capacity

You're looking at 60+ hours of committed time each week before you even add in the gym, meal prep, wedding planning, or a social life. And you're managing POTS and lingering Lyme symptoms on top of it. The question isn't whether you *want* to train and prep meals—it's whether the system you're building can actually hold up under that load without making you sicker or more exhausted.

The answer isn't to do everything perfectly. It's to build a version that fits the time and energy you actually have, not the time and energy you wish you had.

## Start with the minimum effective dose for strength training

Four strength sessions a week is a solid target when you have the time and recovery capacity for it. But when you're managing chronic health conditions and a packed schedule, the [CDC recommends](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/chronic-health-conditions-and-disabilities.html) that adults with chronic conditions aim for muscle-strengthening activities at least two days per week—and emphasizes that *some* activity is better than none.

You don't need to abandon your goals, but you do need a plan that doesn't require perfect conditions to work. Two or three well-structured full-body sessions will build muscle and support fat loss, especially if you're new to consistent training or returning after a break. You can always add a fourth session during lighter weeks, but starting with two or three means you're not constantly playing catch-up or feeling like you're failing when life gets in the way.

Shorter sessions help, too. A 45-minute workout that you actually complete beats an ambitious 90-minute plan you can't sustain. The [CDC notes](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) that activity can be broken into smaller chunks throughout the week—what matters is the cumulative effort, not doing it all at once.

## Meal prep doesn't have to mean Sunday marathons

The idea that meal prep requires a full day of cooking every weekend is one of the biggest barriers to actually doing it. You don't need to batch-cook every meal for the week in one go.

Instead, think in terms of components, not full meals. Cook a big batch of protein (chicken thighs, ground turkey, tofu), roast a sheet pan of vegetables, and cook a large pot of rice or quinoa. Store them separately. You can mix and match throughout the week, which gives you variety without requiring a new recipe every time. The [USDA's time-saving meal prep guidance](https://www.myplate.gov/sites/default/files/2024-09/MyPlate-Partner-Resource-Healthy-Eating-And-Savings-Time-Saving.pdf) emphasizes reducing prep, cooking, and cleanup time by preparing ingredients in bulk and using simple cooking methods—exactly this approach.

Another option: split your prep across two shorter sessions instead of one long one. Thirty minutes on Sunday and thirty minutes on Wednesday is often more sustainable than three hours straight, especially when you're already tired from a long shift or a full day of classes and field hours.

If you're aiming for fat loss and muscle gain, protein is the priority. [Research-backed protein targets for active women](/articles/protein-targets-for-women/) typically land around 0.7–1 gram per pound of body weight. Hitting that target consistently matters more than perfecting every other macro or eating from a complicated recipe.

## Build decision-making into the system, not into your day

Decision fatigue is real, and it compounds when you're managing a packed schedule and chronic health conditions. The fewer decisions you have to make in the moment—what to cook, what to eat, whether to go to the gym—the more likely you are to follow through.

That means deciding *once* what your week looks like, then executing without relitigating the plan every day. Pick two or three training days at the start of the week and put them in your calendar like you would a work shift. Choose one or two meal prep windows and treat them the same way. [MyPlate's meal planning resource](https://www.myplate.gov/eathealthy/budget/budget-weekly-meals) walks through how to plan weekly meals and shop strategically so you're not deciding what to cook every single night.

For meals, a rotation of three to four simple, repeatable options is easier to sustain than trying to cook something new every time. It's not glamorous, but it works—and it frees up mental space for the things that actually require creative energy, like wedding planning or keeping up with six classes.

## What to do when your body says no

POTS and chronic Lyme don't follow a predictable schedule. Some days you'll have the energy for a full training session and meal prep; other days, standing for more than a few minutes might be hard. That variability makes rigid plans backfire.

Instead of a fixed four-day training plan, build in flexibility from the start. Decide on your two non-negotiable training days—the bare minimum that keeps you progressing—and treat a third or fourth session as a bonus when you're feeling up to it. On days when a full session isn't realistic, a ten-minute walk or a few sets of bodyweight squats still counts. The goal is to keep the habit alive, not to hit the same intensity every time.

The same goes for meal prep. If a thirty-minute cooking session feels like too much, fallback options matter. Keep high-protein, low-effort foods on hand—Greek yogurt, rotisserie chicken, canned beans, pre-washed greens, frozen vegetables. They're not as cheap as cooking from scratch, but they're cheaper than takeout and they keep you on track when energy is low.

## Nine months is enough time—if you don't burn out first

You've got nine months until your wedding. That's genuinely enough time to see meaningful changes in body composition *if* the plan you build is one you can actually sustain. The biggest risk isn't that you won't work hard enough—it's that you'll try to do too much, burn out, and lose weeks or months to exhaustion or a flare-up.

Start with the minimum version that keeps you moving forward: two or three strength sessions, one or two short meal-prep windows, and a simple rotation of meals that doesn't require a new recipe every time. You can always add more when you have the capacity. But a plan that survives a bad week is worth more than a perfect plan that collapses the first time something goes wrong.
