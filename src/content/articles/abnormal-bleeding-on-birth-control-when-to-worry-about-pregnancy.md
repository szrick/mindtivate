You're eight months into birth control, bleeding irregularly, using condoms, getting negative pregnancy tests — and still worried you might somehow be pregnant. That spiral is exhausting, and you're not alone in it. Abnormal bleeding on hormonal contraception is one of the most common side effects, but when you've heard stories about people who didn't know they were pregnant, it's hard to trust the reassurance.

Let's break down what's actually happening, what the test results mean, and when bleeding on birth control is genuinely cause for concern.

## Why breakthrough bleeding happens on hormonal birth control

Breakthrough bleeding — bleeding or spotting between expected periods — is especially common in the first several months of starting a hormonal method. [The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) notes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/experts-and-stories/the-latest/what-you-should-know-about-breakthrough-bleeding-with-birth-control) that it happens because your uterine lining is adjusting to lower, steadier hormone levels than it's used to. Instead of the usual rise and fall of estrogen and progesterone that comes with ovulation, hormonal birth control keeps those levels relatively flat.

For most people, breakthrough bleeding improves after the first three to six months. Eight months in, you're past that initial adjustment window, which can feel frustrating — but it doesn't automatically mean something is wrong. Missed pills, certain medications, smoking, and even stress can all trigger irregular bleeding on birth control.

## What a negative urine test actually tells you

You mentioned your test result was "<1" — that means the test detected less than 1 mIU/mL of hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin, the hormone pregnancy tests look for). A standard urine test turns positive around 25 mIU/mL, and most can detect pregnancy within a week or two after a missed period. If you're using condoms consistently *and* on birth control, your pregnancy risk is already very low. A negative test on top of that is strong evidence you're not pregnant.

The "hook effect" you mentioned is real but extremely rare. It happens when hCG levels are so high — typically in the tens or hundreds of thousands — that they overwhelm the test and produce a false negative. That scenario usually only occurs late in pregnancy or with certain medical conditions like molar pregnancy. [Planned Parenthood's guidance on pregnancy symptoms](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/pregnancy/pregnancy-symptoms) emphasizes that if you're not experiencing other signs of pregnancy (nausea, breast tenderness, fatigue, missed periods entirely) and tests are negative, pregnancy is unlikely.

## When abnormal bleeding needs follow-up

You're already scheduled for an ultrasound, which is the right next step. The [CDC's contraceptive guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/pdfs/rr7303a1-H.pdf) recommends clinical evaluation for bleeding that's heavy, prolonged, or doesn't improve after a few months — not because it's usually dangerous, but to rule out other causes like fibroids, polyps, infection, or (rarely) a problem with how the contraceptive is working.

An ultrasound will show whether there's a structural reason for the bleeding and can definitively confirm whether you're pregnant. If the ultrasound and exam are normal, the next conversation is usually about whether to stick with your current method, adjust the dose or formulation, or try something else entirely.

## What to do while you wait

The anxiety loop — bleeding, testing, googling, testing again — is hard to break, especially when you've heard stories that make it seem like negative tests can't be trusted. But the evidence here is on your side: you're using two methods, your tests are negative, and you don't feel pregnant. That combination makes pregnancy extremely unlikely.

If the bleeding itself is bothersome (heavy enough to interfere with daily life, or accompanied by pain), mention that specifically at your ultrasound appointment. [Hormonal birth control can sometimes affect mood](/articles/how-birth-control-can-affect-mood/), and the anxiety around bleeding and pregnancy scares is worth naming too — not because it's "all in your head," but because it's a real side effect of living with unpredictable bleeding for months.

The ultrasound will give you a clear answer. In the meantime, the most likely explanation is that your body is still adjusting to the hormones, not that you're pregnant and don't know it.
