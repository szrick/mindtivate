"Just be consistent" shows up in every comment section about fitness,
and it's technically true — consistency is what works. It's also not a
plan. It doesn't tell you what to do on the Tuesday you're exhausted and
the gym feels impossible.

## Motivation was never the mechanism

Motivation is a feeling, and feelings are unreliable by design — they
respond to sleep, stress, and how your day went, not to your goals. A
plan that depends on motivation being present is a plan that only works
on your best days. The habit-building research points somewhere more
useful: reduce how much a decision is required in the moment at all.

## Lower the bar for "it counts"

One of the more consistent findings in habit research is that people who
give themselves a genuinely small minimum version of a habit stick with
it longer than people aiming for an ideal version every time. If the
plan is "45-minute workout or nothing," a bad day becomes nothing. If the
plan is "45 minutes, or 10 minutes counts," a bad day still becomes
something — and something is what keeps the habit alive until a better
day comes back around.

## Attach it to something that already happens

Habits are easier to keep when they're anchored to an existing routine
rather than a stretch of open time you have to remember to protect.
"After I make coffee, I do my mobility routine" is easier to follow than
"sometime in the morning." The existing habit becomes the cue, so you're
not relying on memory or motivation to notice it's time.

## Expect the interruption, don't treat it as failure

Almost every real habit has a gap in it somewhere — a sick week, travel,
a genuinely bad stretch. The research on habit formation is fairly clear
that a missed day doesn't meaningfully damage a habit; what matters is
whether you return to it soon after, not whether the streak stayed
unbroken. Treating one missed session as proof the habit failed is often
what actually ends it.

## A more useful version of "be consistent"

Define your real minimum, attach it to something you already do, and
plan for the fact that some weeks will be worse than others. That's not
as motivating a sentence as "just be consistent" — but it's the version
that's actually still working three months from now.
