## Why being stared at feels different when you're autistic

You're not imagining it, and you're not overreacting. When someone stares at you repeatedly over months, it's uncomfortable for anyone—but when you're autistic, the experience often carries extra weight. [Research on social anxiety in autistic adults](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7482774/) shows that uncertainty about others' intentions and difficulty reading social cues can amplify distress in situations that neurotypical people might brush off more easily. You don't know *why* he's staring, you can't predict what it means, and that ambiguity itself becomes a source of ongoing anxiety.

The instinct to avoid direct confrontation makes sense, too. You're weighing real social risks: the possibility he'll deny it, twist the narrative, or tell others you're interested in him. That's not paranoia—it's pattern recognition. Women (autistic or not) face these dynamics all the time, and [the stakes feel higher in a small cohort like med school](/articles/how-to-deal-with-a-work-crush-without-derailing-your-career/) where you'll see the same people for years.

## Why your indirect attempt didn't work

You already tried asking if there's an issue without naming the staring directly. That's a reasonable first move—it signals discomfort without full confrontation. But indirect communication often misfires, especially across neurotypes. He may not have connected your question to his behavior at all. Or he understood perfectly well and chose to ignore the hint because it gave him plausible deniability.

Either way, you're left in the same spot: the staring continues, your anxiety hasn't decreased, and now you've used up your "soft" option. That doesn't mean you did anything wrong—it means the situation requires a more direct boundary.

## What a direct boundary actually sounds like

Direct doesn't have to mean aggressive or emotional. It means naming the specific behavior and stating clearly what you want to change. A script that works here:

*"I've noticed you look at me a lot during class. It makes me uncomfortable. I need you to stop."*

That's it. No explanation of why it bothers you, no apology for bringing it up, no softening with "I don't know if you realize" or "maybe it's just me." You're not asking if there's a problem—you're stating that *his behavior* is the problem and needs to stop.

A short, rehearsed script removes some of the cognitive load of improvising in the moment and reduces the chance of being derailed by his reaction. Many autistic people find that having exact words prepared—rather than a general sense of what to say—makes difficult conversations more manageable.

If he denies it or tries to flip it ("I wasn't staring, why would you think that?"), you don't need to defend your perception. A simple repeat works: *"Whether it's intentional or not, I'm asking you to stop."* Then end the conversation. Walk away if you need to.

## When and where to say it

Timing matters for safety and effectiveness. Don't do this when you're alone with him in an empty hallway or after class when no one's around. Choose a moment when other people are nearby but not listening—before class starts, during a break, in a common area. That gives you witnesses without an audience.

If the idea of a face-to-face conversation feels unmanageable, a brief text or message is a legitimate alternative. Written communication has advantages: you control the exact wording, you have a record of what you said, and you don't have to manage his immediate reaction in real time. The same script works in writing.

## What to do if it continues

If he keeps staring after you've named it directly, that's when you escalate to someone with authority. A course director or student affairs office can help you document the pattern and determine next steps, including whether it rises to the level of formal harassment under your school's policies. You don't have to make that determination yourself—that's their job.

You're not required to give him multiple chances or assume good intent indefinitely. One clear boundary is enough. If he ignores it, the problem is his behavior, not your communication style.

## The fear of being misunderstood is real, but it's not a reason to stay silent

The risk you're worried about—that he'll tell people you're interested in him—exists whether you say something or not. But staying silent guarantees the staring continues and your anxiety stays high.

Research on autistic self-advocacy in healthcare settings points out that clear, concrete communication of distress and boundaries reduces frustration and increases the likelihood of being taken seriously. You have the right to name what's happening and ask for it to stop. That's not an accusation of attraction—it's a boundary. Anyone who twists it into something else is showing you exactly who they are.

You don't need to be confident or comfortable to set a boundary. You just need to say it once, clearly, and mean it. After that, what he does with it is his responsibility, not yours.
