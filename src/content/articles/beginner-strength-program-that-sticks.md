The hardest part of a beginner strength program was never the exercises —
it's the third week, when the novelty wears off and you have to decide
whether to show up anyway.

## Start smaller than feels reasonable

Most beginner programs are written by people who already love training,
so they default to five days a week, an hour a session. If you're
starting from zero, that's not a program — it's a plan to quit by week
three. Two or three sessions a week, 30–40 minutes each, is enough to
build real strength in your first few months. You can always add more
once showing up stops requiring willpower.

## Four movements cover almost everything

You don't need twelve exercises to get stronger. A simple split covers
the whole body:

- **A squat pattern** (goblet squat, leg press, or bodyweight squat)
- **A hinge pattern** (Romanian deadlift or hip thrust)
- **A push** (push-up, dumbbell press, or machine chest press)
- **A pull** (row or lat pulldown)

Two to three sets of each, in the 8–12 rep range, is plenty when you're
new. The goal for the first two months isn't intensity — it's building
the habit of showing up and moving well.

## Progress by small increments, not big jumps

Add weight only when the current weight feels clearly manageable for all
your sets — a small plate, not a dramatic jump. Progress that feels
almost too easy to notice is the kind that's still there in six months.

## What actually predicts whether people stick with it

Not motivation. Not the "best" program. The two biggest factors are
whether the gym (or home setup) is convenient, and whether the first few
sessions feel doable rather than crushing. If a session leaves you too
sore to move for four days, you've made next week's session less likely
to happen, not more effective.

## A simple way to start this week

Pick two or three days you can realistically protect. Do the four
movements above, two to three sets each, and stop with something left in
the tank. Repeat next week with slightly more weight or a rep or two
more. That's the whole program — it's meant to be boring enough to keep
doing.
