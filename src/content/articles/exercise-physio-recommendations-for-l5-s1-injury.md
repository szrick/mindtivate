## Why L5/S1 injuries need a specific approach

The L5/S1 disc sits at the very bottom of your lumbar spine, where it meets the sacrum. It bears more load than any other disc in your back, which is why herniations and bulges here are so common—and why they often come with radiating leg pain (sciatica) when the disc presses on a nerve root.

[Most L5/S1 disc injuries improve without surgery](/articles/disc-replacement-vs-microdiscectomy-what-it-means-when-your-surgeon-skips-the-sm/), though understanding your surgical options matters if conservative treatment doesn't work. [Clinical guidelines recommend exercise and structured physical therapy as first-line treatment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations) for low back pain with or without sciatica. But the type of exercise matters—random stretching or core work won't necessarily help, and some movements can make things worse in the short term.

## What "centralization" means (and why it's the goal)

Physical therapists use a concept called *centralization* to guide treatment for disc injuries. If your pain moves from your leg or buttock closer to your lower back during or after an exercise, that's centralization—a sign the nerve is being decompressed and you're moving in the right direction. If pain spreads further down your leg (peripheralization), that movement isn't helping right now.

The McKenzie Method, a widely used PT approach for disc problems, is built around this principle. You're assessed to find your "directional preference"—the specific movements or positions that centralize your pain. For many people with L5/S1 herniations, extension exercises (backward bending) help push the disc material away from the nerve. But not everyone responds to extension; some do better with flexion or other strategies, which is why an individualized assessment matters.

## Extension exercises: prone press-ups and progressions

If extension is your directional preference, you'll likely start with gentle prone lying—just lying on your stomach for a few minutes to see how your symptoms respond. If that goes well, you progress to propping yourself on your elbows (a sphinx position), then to prone press-ups, where you push your upper body up while keeping your hips and legs relaxed on the floor.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons' spine conditioning program outlines a structured progression of stretching, strengthening, and core exercises after lumbar injury. Extension work is often paired with core stabilization once acute symptoms settle—not as a replacement, but as a complement.

You're looking for pain that stays the same or improves during the exercise, and especially for centralization afterward. If leg pain worsens or spreads, stop and reassess with a PT.

## Core stabilization and motor control training

Once the acute nerve irritation calms down, the focus shifts to rebuilding stability. L5/S1 injuries often come with changes in how your deep spinal muscles (transverse abdominis, multifidus) activate—they may fire late or not at all, leaving the spine less supported during everyday movement.

Core stabilization exercises retrain that coordination. Think: pelvic tilts to find a neutral spine position, dead bugs or bird dogs (opposite arm and leg lifts from hands and knees), planks, and bridges. These aren't about building six-pack abs; they're about motor control—teaching your nervous system to stabilize your spine before you move a limb or shift your weight.

## What the research says about timelines

Most people with lumbar disc herniations see significant improvement within six to twelve weeks of structured physical therapy. Pain often decreases faster than function fully returns—you might feel better in a few weeks, but getting back to heavy lifting, running, or other high-load activities usually takes three to four months.

Research published in the *Annals of Internal Medicine* supports exercise and multidisciplinary rehabilitation as effective noninvasive options for acute, subacute, and chronic low back pain. The evidence is strongest for staying active and using structured, supervised exercise rather than bed rest or passive treatments alone.

Spontaneous regression—the disc material actually shrinking on its own—happens in about 75% of herniations within six months. Your body has a decent chance of cleaning up the problem if you can manage symptoms and keep moving safely in the meantime.

## When to see a physical therapist (and what to ask)

If you're dealing with an L5/S1 injury, a PT evaluation is worth it. They'll assess your movement patterns, test for centralization, and build a program tailored to your specific presentation. Not all disc herniations behave the same way, and a cookie-cutter exercise list from the internet won't account for your directional preference or current irritability level.

Ask about:

- Whether your symptoms centralize with any particular movement or position  
- What your directional preference is (if one is clear)  
- How to progress exercises as pain improves  
- What signs mean you should back off versus push through  

Physical therapy isn't just about the exercises you do in the clinic. The AAOS notes that aerobic activity—walking, swimming—and a home program to keep your back and abdominal muscles strong and flexible are central to long-term management.

## The bottom line

L5/S1 injuries respond well to the right kind of movement. Extension exercises work for many people, but not everyone—centralization is the signal that tells you whether a movement is helping. Core stabilization and motor control work rebuild the stability you need to get back to normal life without re-injury. Most people improve significantly in six to twelve weeks with a structured program, and the majority of disc herniations shrink on their own over time. A physical therapist can assess your specific pattern and guide the progression, which beats guessing on your own.
