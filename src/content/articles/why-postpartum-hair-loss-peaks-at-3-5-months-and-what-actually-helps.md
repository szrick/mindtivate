## Why you're shedding so much hair right now

If you're watching clumps of hair circle the shower drain or pulling fistfuls out of your ponytail holder at five months postpartum, you're not imagining it—and you're not alone. What you're experiencing is [telogen effluvium](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/new-moms), a type of temporary hair loss triggered by the hormonal drop after delivery.

During pregnancy, elevated estrogen keeps more of your hair in the growing phase. You shed less than usual, so your hair feels thicker and fuller. After birth, estrogen plummets, and all those hairs that were "on hold" shift into the shedding phase at once. The result? A few months later—typically around three to five months postpartum, right where you are now—you lose what feels like an alarming amount of hair in a very short window.

It's not that you're losing *more* hair than normal over the long run. You're just catching up on all the shedding you didn't do while you were pregnant.

## What the research says about supplements

Biotin and collagen supplements are everywhere in postpartum hair-loss conversations, but the evidence for them is thin. [Postpartum telogen effluvium doesn't have a true curative treatment](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/23297-postpartum-hair-loss)—it's a self-limiting process that resolves on its own, usually by your baby's first birthday.

Biotin deficiency *can* cause hair loss, and some studies have found low biotin levels in a meaningful percentage of women experiencing hair shedding. But here's the catch: serum biotin isn't a reliable marker of true deficiency, and controlled trials haven't shown consistent benefit from supplementation unless there's a confirmed deficiency from malabsorption or other specific causes. If you're eating a reasonably varied diet, adding biotin when you're not actually deficient won't speed up regrowth.

Collagen is similar. Your body breaks dietary collagen down into peptides and amino acids during digestion—and while some research suggests these peptides may have signaling effects beyond just providing raw materials, there's no strong evidence they help with postpartum shedding specifically. Some small studies hint at benefits for hair thickness or skin elasticity, but the data isn't robust enough to call collagen a solution for this particular type of hair loss.

That said, if you *are* dealing with a nutritional gap—low iron, inadequate protein intake, or a restrictive diet while breastfeeding—that can worsen hair loss or delay recovery. It's worth checking in with your provider about bloodwork (especially ferritin, which reflects your iron stores) if your shedding feels extreme or if you're exhausted, pale, or dealing with other symptoms that might point to a deficiency.

## How to get through the shedding phase

Since postpartum hair loss is driven by hormones, not a lack of nutrients or products, the most honest answer is: time. The shedding will slow as your hormone levels stabilize, and regrowth will follow. Most people see noticeable improvement by around 12 months postpartum.

These strategies won't speed up the process, but they can make it less distressing and protect the hair you still have:

**Avoid tight hairstyles and harsh treatments.** Pulling your hair back into tight ponytails, buns, or braids adds tension to already-fragile hair and can worsen breakage or even cause traction alopecia. Heat styling, chemical treatments, and rough brushing can also snap off new regrowth before it has a chance to gain length.

**Use a gentle shampoo and a wide-tooth comb.** Treat your hair like it's delicate, because right now it is. Detangle carefully when it's wet (hair is most vulnerable to breakage then), and avoid scrubbing your scalp aggressively.

**Consider a shorter cut or different part.** This won't stop the shedding, but it can make your hair look fuller and reduce the visual impact of thinner areas. A side part can cover a more visible hairline; layers can add volume.

**Don't panic if you see short "baby hairs" around your hairline.** Those are new growth—a sign that the process is moving in the right direction.

If your hair loss continues past 12 months postpartum, or if you're noticing bald patches, scalp irritation, or other symptoms that don't fit the typical pattern, check in with a dermatologist. [Telogen effluvium is temporary and diffuse](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6709511/); if something else is going on—thyroid dysfunction, an autoimmune condition, or another trigger—you'll want to rule that out.

## The takeaway

Postpartum hair loss at five months is right on schedule, and as brutal as it feels, it's a sign that your body is doing exactly what it's supposed to do hormonally. Supplements might help if you have an underlying deficiency, but they won't override the normal shedding process. What will help: patience, gentle handling, and the knowledge that this phase is temporary. The regrowth is already on its way.
