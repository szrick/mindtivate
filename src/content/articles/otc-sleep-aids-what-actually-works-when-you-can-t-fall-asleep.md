## When sleepless nights start causing headaches

You're lying awake again, head pounding from another night of too little sleep, wondering if there's something—anything—you can take without a prescription that will help you actually fall asleep. The exhaustion is piling up, and you need relief.

Over-the-counter sleep aids are everywhere, from melatonin gummies to antihistamine pills to magnesium supplements. But they work in different ways, and not all of them are backed by strong evidence. Here's what each one does, what the research actually shows, and when they might (or might not) help.

## Melatonin: the hormone that signals nighttime

Melatonin is a hormone your body naturally produces when it gets dark, signaling that it's time to sleep. Taking a supplement adds a little extra to that signal.

The evidence is mixed but generally positive for certain types of sleep problems. [Research shows](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know) that melatonin can reduce the time it takes to fall asleep by about seven to nine minutes on average and may improve sleep quality slightly. That's a modest effect—don't expect it to knock hours off your tossing and turning—but for some people, those few minutes make a real difference. Melatonin works best for people with a delayed sleep schedule—night owls who need to fall asleep earlier than their body wants to—or for jet lag.

For [chronic insomnia where you're lying awake with racing thoughts or waking up repeatedly](/articles/waking-too-early-and-needing-8-5-hours-is-it-insomnia/), melatonin's effects are more modest. It's not a sedative, so it won't knock you out. Think of it as nudging your internal clock rather than forcing sleep.

**Dosing and timing matter more than you'd think.** Most store-bought melatonin comes in 3 mg, 5 mg, or even 10 mg doses, but research suggests that 0.5 mg to 5 mg is the effective range—and higher doses don't work better. Start with 0.5 to 1 mg taken 1 to 3 hours before your intended bedtime. Recent meta-analyses suggest melatonin is more effective when taken earlier in the evening rather than right before you want to sleep, giving it time to work with your circadian rhythm. If that doesn't help after a week, you can increase by 1 mg increments, but there's little reason to go above 5 mg.

One thing to watch: melatonin can cause headaches in some people, which is the opposite of what you need right now. Other mild side effects include daytime grogginess, nausea, or vivid dreams.

## Magnesium: the mineral linked to relaxation

Magnesium plays a role in muscle relaxation and nervous system regulation, and low magnesium levels have been associated with poor sleep. Supplementing with magnesium—especially magnesium bisglycinate (also called glycinate) or magnesium citrate—has shown some promise for improving sleep onset and quality.

A few small studies found that magnesium supplementation (typically 250 to 500 mg) reduced the time it took to fall asleep by about 17 minutes and showed a trend toward increased total sleep time, though that finding wasn't statistically significant. One study also found improvements in sleep efficiency and reductions in cortisol, a stress hormone that can keep you wired at night.

That said, the evidence is still limited, and results vary depending on the form of magnesium and the population studied. Magnesium appears most helpful for people who are low in magnesium to begin with—something that's hard to know without testing. It's generally safe, though higher doses can cause digestive upset.

If you want to try it, magnesium bisglycinate is gentler on the stomach and well-absorbed. Magnesium citrate is another reasonably absorbed option, though it can cause loose stools at higher doses. You may have seen magnesium L-threonate marketed for brain health—it has some emerging research on cognition, but the human evidence for sleep specifically is limited. Start with 200 to 250 mg in the evening and give it a few weeks to see if it makes a difference.

## Antihistamines: the drowsy side effect turned sleep aid

Many over-the-counter sleep aids—like Benadryl, Unisom, or the "PM" versions of pain relievers—contain antihistamines such as diphenhydramine or doxylamine. These drugs were originally designed to treat allergies, but they make you drowsy by blocking histamine receptors in the brain that promote wakefulness.

Antihistamines can help you fall asleep faster in the short term, but the [National Institutes of Health cautions](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/treatment) against using them regularly. The sedative effect wears off quickly as your body builds tolerance, meaning they become less effective within days to weeks. They also disrupt sleep architecture—you might fall asleep, but the quality of that sleep is often poor, leaving you groggy and foggy the next day.

Side effects include dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and daytime drowsiness that can linger well into the morning. For older adults, antihistamines carry additional risks, including confusion and an increased risk of falls.

If you're going to use an antihistamine, keep it short-term—a few nights, not every night—and expect diminishing returns.

## What about the headaches?

The headaches you're experiencing are likely tension headaches or a symptom of sleep deprivation itself. Chronic lack of sleep disrupts your body's stress response and can trigger or worsen headaches. Ironically, some sleep aids—particularly melatonin and antihistamines—list headache as a potential side effect, which could make things worse rather than better.

If you try a supplement and your headaches increase or don't improve after a week or two of better sleep, that's a sign to stop and reassess.

## When supplements aren't enough

If over-the-counter options don't help after a few weeks, or if your insomnia is chronic (lasting more than three months and happening most nights), it's worth talking to a doctor. [Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)](/articles/when-sleep-medications-stop-working-what-cbt-i-treats-that-pills-don-t/) is considered the gold standard treatment because it addresses the underlying patterns keeping you awake—racing thoughts, anxiety about sleep, irregular schedules—rather than just masking the symptoms.

Prescription sleep medications are another option for short-term use, but they come with their own risks, including dependence and rebound insomnia when you stop.

## The bottom line

Melatonin and magnesium are the most evidence-backed over-the-counter options for occasional sleep trouble, though neither is a magic bullet. Melatonin works best for circadian rhythm issues, while magnesium may help if you're deficient or have trouble winding down at night. Antihistamines can help in a pinch but aren't a sustainable solution.

Start with the lowest effective dose of whichever you choose, give it at least a week to work, and pay attention to how you feel during the day. If your sleep doesn't improve—or if those headaches persist—it's time to dig deeper with professional help rather than cycling through more bottles from the drugstore.
