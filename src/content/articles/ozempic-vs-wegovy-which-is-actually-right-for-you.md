## They're the same drug — so why the different names?

If you've spent any time in a weight-loss forum lately, you've seen both names thrown around like they're competing products: Ozempic *or* Wegovy, pick a side. Here's the thing that gets lost in that framing — they're the same active ingredient, made by the same company, and the real differences come down to dose, what they're actually FDA-approved for, and what that means for your insurance and your prescription. Neither is objectively "better." Which one makes sense for you depends on specifics, not brand loyalty.

This isn't the influencer-hype version of that answer. It's the one built on what the FDA actually approved, what the trials actually measured, and what changes for women specifically — fertility, muscle mass, and the insurance maze — that a lot of comparison posts skip entirely.

## What is Ozempic?

Ozempic is a brand name for semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, approved by the FDA in December 2017 for adults with type 2 diabetes — to improve blood sugar control, and later expanded to reduce cardiovascular and kidney risk in people who already have diabetes. It comes in 0.5 mg, 1 mg, and 2 mg once-weekly doses, [per its FDA prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/209637s020s021lbl.pdf). Weight loss is a genuine, well-documented side effect of Ozempic — it's just not what the drug is approved to treat. When a provider prescribes it specifically for weight loss in someone without diabetes, that's an off-label use, which is legal and common, but it changes how insurance treats the prescription (more on that below).

## What is Wegovy?

Wegovy is also semaglutide, made by the same company (Novo Nordisk), but formulated and approved specifically for chronic weight management. The FDA approved it in June 2021 — [the first new obesity treatment approved since 2014](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USFDA/bulletins/2e2adc7) — for adults with obesity (BMI ≥30) or overweight (BMI ≥27) with at least one weight-related condition like high blood pressure or high cholesterol. Wegovy is dosed higher: it escalates up to 2.4 mg once weekly, above Ozempic's 2 mg ceiling, because that's the dose the weight-loss trials were actually built around.

## Key differences that actually matter

**Dose.** Wegovy's approved maintenance dose (2.4 mg) is higher than Ozempic's maximum (2 mg). This isn't a marketing distinction — the dose-response relationship for semaglutide and weight loss is real, and the extra 0.4 mg is part of why Wegovy tends to outperform Ozempic for weight loss specifically, even though the molecule is identical.

**What each one is actually approved for.** Ozempic: type 2 diabetes (plus cardiovascular/kidney risk reduction for people who have it). Wegovy: chronic weight management. Using Ozempic for weight loss alone is off-label; using Wegovy for diabetes control isn't its approved use either. This matters more than it sounds like it should, because approval status drives insurance decisions.

**Real-world weight-loss results.** In the STEP 1 trial, people without diabetes taking Wegovy lost an average of about 15% of body weight over 68 weeks, versus roughly 2% on placebo. The [STEP 5 trial's two-year follow-up](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9556320/) found a similar result held up over time — about 15% average loss sustained at 104 weeks. Ozempic's own trials, run in people with diabetes at its lower max dose, generally show more modest weight loss — diabetes itself, and the lower ceiling, both play a role.

**Cost and insurance — this is where it gets genuinely frustrating.** Both drugs are expensive without coverage, and pricing has been shifting: [Novo Nordisk announced list price cuts](https://www.cnn.com/2026/02/24/health/novo-nordisk-ozempic-wegovy-costs) that bring both medications down significantly, with further reductions planned. But list price isn't what most people actually pay — insurance coverage is the real deciding factor, and it splits along the same approval lines as everything else. Ozempic is far more likely to be covered by commercial insurance and Medicare Part D when you have a type 2 diabetes diagnosis. Wegovy coverage for weight loss varies enormously by plan — some cover it with prior authorization, some exclude weight-loss medications outright, and Medicare has historically not covered GLP-1s for weight loss alone (only for diabetes or, more recently, specific cardiovascular indications). If you don't have diabetes, getting Ozempic prescribed and covered specifically for weight loss can be harder than it sounds, even though the drug itself is identical to what a diabetic patient down the hall is taking.

## What's different for women specifically

**Fertility and unplanned pregnancy.** This one catches people off guard. Semaglutide slows gastric emptying — the same mechanism that curbs appetite — and there's real concern that this can interfere with how completely oral birth control pills get absorbed, which may reduce their effectiveness. On top of that, for women with PCOS, semaglutide's effect on insulin sensitivity can restore ovulation that wasn't happening reliably before, which is exactly why the term "Ozempic babies" started circulating. Neither drug is safe during pregnancy — [research on incretin receptor agonists during pregnancy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12794308/) points to risks of preterm birth and other complications — and the general guidance is to stop at least two months before trying to conceive. If you're on either drug and using oral birth control, this is worth a direct conversation with your provider about backup contraception, not something to assume is a non-issue. **Muscle mass.** Roughly a quarter to a third of the weight lost on a GLP-1 is lean mass, not fat — and [a narrative review specifically on GLP-1 use in women](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/4/632) flags this as a bigger concern for women, especially post-menopause, where muscle and bone density are already harder to maintain. This isn't a reason to avoid either drug, but it is a reason to treat resistance training and adequate protein as part of the plan, not an optional add-on. **It's not just appetite suppression.** Both drugs work through more than just "you feel less hungry" — slower digestion, changed food preferences, and gut-hormone signaling are all part of it. ## Which should you choose?

If you have type 2 diabetes, Ozempic is the more straightforward path — it's what it's approved and typically covered for, and you'll get cardiovascular and kidney benefits built into the same prescription. If weight loss is your primary goal and you don't have diabetes, Wegovy is the drug actually built and dosed for that purpose, and it's what a provider is more likely to prescribe on-label for an obesity or overweight-plus-comorbidity diagnosis. Some providers still prescribe Ozempic off-label for weight loss, often because of cost, availability, or insurance quirks specific to your plan — that's a legitimate conversation to have, just go in knowing it's off-label rather than assuming it's interchangeable with Wegovy on paper.

## The takeaway

Ozempic and Wegovy aren't really competing products — they're the same medication split across two different approved uses, with Wegovy dosed higher specifically for weight loss. The right choice isn't about which one is "stronger" or trendier; it's about your actual diagnosis, what your insurance will cover, and a plan for the parts that don't show up in a before-and-after photo — birth control reliability, muscle preservation, and what happens if you want to get pregnant later. Bring the specifics — your labs, your insurance plan, your reproductive plans — to that conversation with your provider, rather than a brand name you saw in someone's comment section.
