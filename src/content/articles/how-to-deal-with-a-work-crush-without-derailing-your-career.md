## When someone else names what you've been noticing

You'd clocked him before—somewhere in the back of your mind—but it took your older colleague's comment to turn a vague awareness into a full-blown crush. Now you're working in a foreign country, navigating office dynamics in English (not your first language, not his either if he's local), and suddenly every interaction feels loaded. You're both 35, presumably single, and the fact that she thought you'd be a good match has made it harder to ignore.

This is one of the trickier versions of workplace attraction: it wasn't entirely organic, and now you're second-guessing whether the feelings are real or just suggestion taking root.

## Why workplace crushes feel so intense

Proximity breeds familiarity, and familiarity can easily tip into attraction—especially when you're spending eight-plus hours a day with someone. Add in the fact that you're living abroad, possibly with a smaller social circle, and a coworker who seems like a good fit can start to feel like the only fit.

There's also the element of scarcity and anticipation. You can't just text him whenever you want or plan a date. Every interaction is brief, semi-public, and constrained by professional norms, which paradoxically makes those moments feel more charged.

But here's the thing: [workplace romances are common](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/workplace-romance)—SHRM's 2025 research found that 52% of U.S. workers are currently in or have previously had a workplace romance—but they also carry real risks. Power dynamics, favoritism, policy violations, and the fallout if things go badly are all legitimate concerns, not just HR paranoia.

## What to do before you do anything

First, check your company's policy. Some organizations have strict rules about dating coworkers, especially if there's any reporting relationship involved (even an indirect one). If you're in different departments with no overlap in authority, that's generally safer, but you need to know what you're working with. [Harvard Business Review's guidance on office romance](https://hbr.org/2019/02/how-to-approach-an-office-romance-and-how-not-to) emphasizes this step—ignoring policy can cost you your job, not just the relationship.

Second, get honest about whether this is attraction or loneliness. Being an expat can be isolating, and it's easy to project a lot onto someone who feels familiar or safe in an unfamiliar environment. That doesn't mean the feelings aren't real, but it's worth asking whether you'd feel the same pull if you had a fuller social life outside work. (If you find yourself [chronically second-guessing your decisions](/articles/how-to-stop-regretting-every-choice-you-ve-made/), that pattern may also be worth examining.)

Third, consider the actual risk. If this goes well, great—but if it goes badly, you still have to see him every day. In a big company that might mean occasional awkward encounters; in a smaller team it could mean daily discomfort. And if your older colleague was playing matchmaker, there's a chance others have noticed too, which adds another layer of visibility (and potential gossip).

## How to manage the feelings in the meantime

You don't have to act on every feeling. Sometimes the healthiest move is to let a crush exist without feeding it—especially while you're still figuring out whether it's mutual, whether it's wise, and whether it's worth the professional risk.

Limit the mental real estate it takes up. Notice when you're replaying conversations or imagining scenarios, and gently redirect. This isn't about suppressing emotion—it's about not letting it eat up your entire headspace during the workday.

Don't change your behavior around him in obvious ways. Seeking him out more, lingering in conversations, or suddenly dressing differently will be visible to others—and 40% of workers view workplace romances as unprofessional, according to SHRM surveys. Perceptions of favoritism or inappropriate behavior can form even before anything actually happens.

And be cautious about talking to the colleague who brought him up. She may have had good intentions, but involving her further (or asking her to relay interest) turns this into office gossip, not a private decision between two adults.

## If you decide to say something

If after reflection you still want to explore this, keep it simple, private, and low-pressure. A casual "Would you want to grab coffee outside of work sometime?" is clear without being a grand declaration. It gives him an easy out if he's not interested, and it keeps the conversation off company property (literally and figuratively).

Don't do this over work email, Slack, or in front of others. And don't do it if you're not prepared for a no—or for the possibility that he says yes and it still doesn't go anywhere.

If he's interested and you move forward, have a conversation early about how you'll handle it at work. Keeping it private (at least initially) protects both of you. And if your company requires disclosure, do it—it's uncomfortable, but it's better than getting caught in a policy violation later.

## If you decide to let it go

Not every crush needs to be pursued. Sometimes the smartest move is to let it fade, especially when the stakes are this high.

If you choose not to act on it, give yourself permission to close that mental chapter rather than leaving it open indefinitely. That might mean consciously deciding "I'm not pursuing this" rather than staying in a vague holding pattern, or it might mean redirecting the energy you've been spending on him toward building friendships outside work—people who can fill some of the social gap that being an expat creates.

That said, if this feels like more than a passing attraction and the circumstances are genuinely low-risk (no power imbalance, no policy violations, and you're both emotionally available), it's not inherently reckless to explore it carefully. Just go in with your eyes open, a clear sense of what you're risking, and a plan for how you'll handle it if it doesn't work out.
