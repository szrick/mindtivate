## What happened in your brain during a blackout

A blackout isn't passing out. It's a period when you're awake, moving, and talking—but your brain has stopped forming new memories. [Alcohol disrupts the hippocampus](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/brochures-and-fact-sheets/interrupted-memories-alcohol-induced-blackouts), the part of your brain responsible for transferring short-term experiences into long-term storage—and [alcohol's effects on the brain](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/brochures-and-fact-sheets/interrupted-memories-alcohol-induced-blackouts) extend beyond memory formation to sleep architecture as well. You can appear conscious and even functional to someone watching you, but inside, nothing is being recorded.

That's why the gap in your memory feels so complete. It's not that you forgot—the memories were never made in the first place. Blackouts typically happen when blood alcohol concentration rises quickly, often after binge drinking or drinking on an empty stomach. Research suggests women may be more vulnerable to blackouts at comparable doses, likely due to differences in body composition and alcohol metabolism, though findings vary across studies.

The fact that you were vomiting and could barely stand means your blood alcohol level was dangerously high—well past the point where cognitive function, judgment, and decision-making are severely impaired.

## The consent question you're asking is the right one

You're struggling with how someone could think you were capable of consenting when you were that visibly intoxicated. That question matters, and it's not one you should have to carry alone.

Ethically, consent requires the ability to understand what's happening and make a decision freely. Alcohol and drugs can make someone unable to consent. If you were vomiting, unable to stand, and blacked out, you were incapacitated—regardless of whether you appeared awake or said words you don't remember.

Legally, the picture is more complicated. Laws differ by state, and some still distinguish between voluntary and involuntary intoxication in ways that can affect how incapacitation is defined in court. But the ethical principle remains: incapacitation means no capacity to consent.

Someone who proceeds with sex under those circumstances made a choice. The fact that you can't remember doesn't mean consent happened. The absence of memory isn't the same as the presence of agreement.

## What you can do right now

First: what happened to you wasn't your fault. Drinking too much doesn't make someone else's actions okay.

If this happened recently—within the last 72 hours to five days, depending on your location—you have the option to get a forensic medical exam (sometimes called a rape kit) and toxicology testing, even if you're not sure yet whether you want to report what happened. The exam collects evidence and checks for injuries; you don't have to decide about pressing charges in order to have one done. Because the window varies by jurisdiction, call RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline (1-800-656-HOPE) or a local hospital to confirm the timeline where you are. RAINN can also connect you with a center that provides these exams at no cost.

You can also talk to someone trained in supporting survivors. RAINN's hotline is confidential and available 24/7. They won't pressure you toward any particular next step—they'll help you think through your options and what feels right for you.

If you're not ready to talk to anyone yet, that's okay too. Trauma responses don't follow a schedule. Some people need time to process alone before reaching out.

## Your relationship with alcohol going forward

This section is about your relationship with alcohol going forward, not about what happened to you. What someone else did is not a drinking problem you can prevent.

That said, if you want to keep drinking, understanding how blackouts happen can help you avoid them. [Rapid increases in blood alcohol concentration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844761/) are the strongest predictor of blackouts—not total amount consumed, but how fast it gets into your bloodstream. Spacing drinks over time, eating before and while you drink, and alternating alcohol with water all slow that rise.

But here's the harder truth: if you're drinking to the point of vomiting and losing the ability to stand, harm reduction strategies might not be enough. Blacking out once doesn't mean you have a problem with alcohol, but it does mean your relationship with drinking right now put you in serious danger—not just from whoever was in your bed, but from injury, assault, and alcohol poisoning itself.

Some people find that after a blackout, they need to step back from drinking entirely for a while, or reassess how and when they drink. Others realize they need more support than they can give themselves. There's no shame in either.

## What comes next is up to you

You're allowed to feel however you feel—sad, angry, confused, numb, all of it at once. You're allowed to take time to figure out what you need. You're allowed to talk to someone, report what happened, or not. You're allowed to change your mind about any of those things.

What you're not allowed to do—what no one gets to do—is blame yourself for someone else's decision to have sex with you when you were incapacitated. You didn't cause this by drinking. The person who saw you in that state and proceeded anyway made a choice, and that choice was theirs.

If you need immediate support, RAINN's hotline is 1-800-656-4673. If you're thinking about your drinking and want to explore that more, your doctor or a therapist who specializes in substance use can help you figure out what's right for you—not from a place of judgment, but from a place of keeping you safe.
