## The real question isn't about the cat

You're one week postpartum in a 960-square-foot house with a cat who pees in closets, a heating problem, and a husband who wants the animal gone. It's easy to frame this as a choice between your pet and your baby's warmth—but that's not actually what's happening here.

Your cat's inappropriate urination isn't spite or stubbornness. It's almost always a signal of an underlying medical issue or significant stress. [Veterinary guidance on managing pets around newborns](https://www.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk491/files/inline-files/Facilitating_Family_Dog_and_Cats_with_Babies.pdf) emphasizes evaluating the behavior itself before assuming separation or rehoming is necessary. A cat who consistently urinates outside the litter box—especially in specific locations like closets—needs a vet visit, not a new home. Urinary tract infections, feline idiopathic cystitis (stress-related bladder inflammation), diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and arthritis can all trigger this behavior. So can anxiety from environmental changes—like, say, a new baby.

If your cat hasn't been examined for this issue, that's step one. Rehoming an animal with an undiagnosed medical or behavioral problem doesn't solve anything; it just transfers the issue to someone else (or leads to the cat being returned or surrendered). And if the root cause is stress from the new baby, removing the stressor—meaning structured separation and environmental adjustments—may actually resolve the behavior without rehoming.

## The heating concern is real, but leaving doors open at night isn't the safest answer

Your bedrooms get cold when the doors are closed. That's uncomfortable, and it's worth addressing—but the solution isn't as simple as leaving doors open overnight.

[Fire safety research](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/fief/) consistently recommends closing bedroom doors before sleeping. A closed door slows the spread of smoke, heat, and toxic gases during a house fire, potentially giving you critical extra minutes to wake, react, and escape. The U.S. Fire Administration specifically advises installing working smoke alarms both inside and outside sleeping areas, precisely because a closed door is a meaningful safety barrier. (The specific "Close Before You Doze" research on this comes from UL's Fire Safety Research Institute, if you want to dig deeper.)

That doesn't mean your baby should sleep in a freezing room. Overheating is a separate [safe sleep risk factor](https://safetosleep.nichd.nih.gov/reduce-risk/reduce), but so is a room cold enough that caregivers are tempted to add loose blankets or unsafe bedding. Most guidance suggests 68–72°F (20–22°C) as a comfortable range for infant sleep—cool enough to reduce overheating risk, warm enough that you're not reaching for extra layers. The answer is better heat distribution during waking hours, a safe sleep environment at the right temperature, and—if doors need to stay open for airflow during the day—a barrier that keeps the cat out of the closets without requiring the door to be fully shut.

## What a tall gate or screen door can (and can't) do

You mentioned wanting a pet gate to allow airflow while keeping the cat out. That's not a bad idea—in theory. Specialty extra-tall gates (some extend to 70+ inches) and reinforced mesh screen doors can create a physical barrier that lets air circulate while preventing a cat from entering. These aren't standard baby gates, which typically top out around 36–41 inches—you'd need to search specifically for "extra-tall pet gates" or "cat-proof gates" to find the taller options. The [ASPCA's guidance on cats and babies](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/cat-care/cats-and-babies) even suggests a screen-door option to keep infant sleeping areas off-limits while maintaining visibility and airflow.

But here's the catch: a gate or screen only works if it actually stops your cat. Some cats won't bother trying to bypass a tall barrier. Others will climb, push, or claw through mesh within days. If your cat is anxious or determined, a gate might just add frustration without solving the problem. And during nighttime sleep, you'd still want bedroom doors fully closed for fire safety—meaning the gate would only help during daytime naps or while you're awake and moving around the house.

A gate isn't a magic fix. It's a tool that works for some households and fails in others, depending on the cat's behavior, the layout, and whether the underlying stressor (the baby, the confined space, an undiagnosed medical issue) is being addressed at the same time.

## Address the behavior, then decide about the cat

Rehoming might ultimately be the right choice—but not as a first response to a problem you haven't yet investigated. You're [one week into life with a newborn](/articles/what-research-says-about-postpartum-return-to-exercise/); this isn't the moment for permanent decisions based on incomplete information. Start with a vet appointment to rule out medical causes. If the issue is behavioral, work with your vet or a veterinary behaviorist to identify stressors and try structured interventions: more litter boxes in different locations, synthetic pheromone diffusers, and environmental enrichment that gives the cat safe spaces away from the baby's activity.

If the behavior doesn't improve after a reasonable trial period—and the cat's stress or your family's safety and sleep are genuinely compromised—then rehoming becomes a more informed, less reactive decision. But right now, you're in the hardest, most sleep-deprived stretch of early parenthood.

Your husband's frustration is understandable. A small house, a new baby, a heating issue, and a pet problem all at once is a lot. (And if you're feeling your own hair-trigger anger or overwhelm right now, [postpartum rage](/articles/postpartum-rage-the-symptoms-no-one-prepares-you-for/) is worth understanding too.) But "get rid of the cat" shortcuts a process that might actually solve the issue—and leaves you wondering later whether you gave up too soon on an animal you cared about. Take the time to figure out what's wrong, try the fixes that match the diagnosis, and make the call from there.
