Eight hours has become the number everyone chases, so it's confusing
when you hit it and still feel wrecked in the morning. Total time
asleep matters, but it's only one input — quality and timing matter
just as much, and either one can quietly undo a technically "full"
night.

## Time asleep isn't the same as quality sleep

Sleep runs in cycles of roughly 90 minutes, moving through lighter and
deeper stages, including REM. Waking up during a light stage feels
very different from waking up mid-deep-sleep, even if the total hours
are identical. Frequent brief awakenings — from light, noise, a
partner, or a pet — can quietly fragment a night without you fully
remembering them, leaving you with eight hours on paper and far less
restorative sleep in practice.

## Consistency matters more than people expect

Going to bed and waking up at wildly different times, even while
still hitting eight hours, disrupts your circadian rhythm — the
internal clock that times your sleep stages and hormone release. A
consistent schedule, even an imperfect one, tends to produce more
restorative sleep than a longer but erratic one.

## The usual suspects worth checking

Alcohol shortens time in REM sleep even when it helps you fall asleep
faster. Late caffeine lingers longer than most people assume — its
half-life is roughly five to six hours. A too-warm room interferes
with the body temperature drop that helps trigger deep sleep. None of
these show up as "not enough hours," but all of them show up as
feeling tired anyway.

## What to actually try first

Before assuming something's medically wrong, it's worth ruling out the
simple stuff: a consistent schedule, cutting off caffeine by early
afternoon, and a cooler room. If you're doing all of that and still
waking up exhausted, that's a reasonable point to bring it up with a
doctor — persistent unrefreshing sleep despite good habits is worth
looking into, not something to just push through indefinitely.
