## It's not age—but it might be how you're spending your days

You're 31, not 71. So why does your back feel like it's giving out already?

The short answer: lower back pain at this age isn't about wear and tear from decades of living. It's more likely about what you're doing (or not doing) right now—how you sit, how much you move, and whether your body's getting the kind of regular, varied activity it needs to stay resilient.

Let's break down what's actually happening and what the evidence says helps.

## Sitting alone isn't the villain—but it's part of the picture

You've probably heard that "sitting is the new smoking." The reality is more nuanced.

[Research on sitting and lower back pain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2200681/) shows that sitting by itself isn't clearly established as a direct cause of back pain. What *does* seem to increase risk is sitting combined with other factors: awkward postures, whole-body vibration (think long commutes or certain machinery), and—crucially—staying in one position for hours without a break. Recent research, including a 2025 scoping review, suggests that sitting *behavior* (static sitting, fewer breaks) has stronger evidence linking it to back pain than total sitting time alone.

If you're working at a desk all day, the issue isn't just the chair. It's that you're holding the same posture for extended periods, your muscles aren't getting the variety of movement they need, and certain areas (like your hip flexors) are staying shortened while others (like your glutes) aren't activating much at all.

A lumbar support cushion can help by encouraging a more neutral spine position and reducing the load on your lower back when you do sit. It's not a fix on its own, but it's a reasonable piece of the puzzle if your current chair setup leaves you slumped or arching uncomfortably.

## Why movement is the most evidence-backed intervention

When your back starts hurting, the instinct is often to rest it—to avoid movement until it feels better.

The research points the other way. [Staying active and incorporating regular movement](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK284944/) is one of the most consistently supported strategies for managing persistent or recurring lower back pain. That doesn't mean pushing through sharp, acute pain or ignoring warning signs—but it does mean that gentle, progressive movement is generally more helpful than prolonged rest.

[Current clinical guidelines](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10508241/) support exercise interventions including trunk strengthening, endurance training, movement-control work, and aerobic activity for managing low back pain in adults. The goal isn't just to "stretch it out"—it's to build the capacity and coordination your body needs to handle the loads you're asking of it every day.

If you've been sedentary and your back is protesting, that's a signal. Not that you're broken, but that your [muscles and joints need regular movement through different ranges and loads](/articles/what-actually-fixes-chronic-neck-and-shoulder-pain/).

## When to take it seriously

Most lower back pain improves with time and movement. But some patterns warrant a closer look.

[Clinical guidance on low back pain evaluation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538173/) highlights red flags that suggest something more serious might be going on: pain that's getting worse instead of better, pain that radiates down your leg with numbness or weakness, bowel or bladder changes, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer or significant trauma.

If your pain fits any of those descriptions, see a healthcare provider sooner rather than later. For garden-variety lower back discomfort that's been building gradually, the first-line approach is usually activity modification, movement, and—if needed—working with a physical therapist to address your specific movement patterns and weaknesses.

## What to do right now

Start with the basics: move more throughout your day. Set a timer to stand and walk every 30–45 minutes if you're desk-bound. One thing worth knowing: individual breaks tend to provide short-term relief rather than an instant fix, so the benefit is cumulative—it's the pattern of regular movement over time that makes the difference.

Incorporate some form of regular strengthening and mobility work. Many physical therapists emphasize targeting your core, hips, and posterior chain rather than your back in isolation, since these muscle groups work together to support your spine.

If sitting is unavoidable for long stretches, a lumbar support cushion can help maintain a more neutral spine position and reduce some of the passive load on your lower back. But it's a tool, not a cure.

And if the pain persists or worsens despite your efforts, don't wait it out indefinitely. [Physical therapy that's tailored to your specific movement limitations](/articles/exercise-physio-recommendations-for-l5-s1-injury/) and goals is far more effective than generic advice or hoping it resolves on its own.

Your back isn't failing you because you've hit some arbitrary age threshold. It's asking for something different than what it's been getting—and the good news is, that's something you can actually address.
