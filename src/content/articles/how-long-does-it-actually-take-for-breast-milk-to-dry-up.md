## The short answer: weeks, not days

You stopped nursing your 14-month-old a week ago. You pumped just enough to take the edge off for a few days, iced when things felt tight, and now—finally—you didn't need to pump at all yesterday or today. Your breasts feel less tender. So when will your milk actually be *gone*?

The timeline varies more than most people expect. Some women feel mostly back to normal within two weeks; others notice occasional leakage or firmness for a month or longer. The NHS notes that milk suppression can take a couple of weeks, with leakage or discomfort lasting 7–14 days—but that's after gradual weaning, not cold turkey. When you stop abruptly, your body still needs time to get the memo.

## Why the six-week estimate keeps coming up

You've probably seen "six weeks" cited as the standard timeline. That figure isn't wrong, but it's also not the whole story. It refers to the broader process of stopping breastfeeding—[which can take weeks to months when you're dropping feeds one at a time](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/how-to-stop/). If you wean gradually, your supply adjusts incrementally. If you stop cold turkey after nursing a 14-month-old (who was likely down to just a few feeds a day), the clock starts ticking from your last nursing session, not your first dropped feed.

Your milk supply operates on feedback inhibition: when milk isn't removed, your body produces a protein (FIL, or feedback inhibitor of lactation) that signals the breast to slow down production. But you were making milk for over a year, so your prolactin receptors are well-established and your ducts are used to regular stimulation. It takes time for that system to fully shut down.

## What you're noticing now is normal

A week out, feeling less tender and not needing to pump is a good sign—you're past the worst of the engorgement phase. But "dried up" doesn't mean your breasts are completely empty or that you'll never express a drop again. Some women can hand-express small amounts of milk months (or even years) after weaning. That's residual milk in the ducts, not active production.

The fact that you combo-fed your first child and only nursed for nine months might mean your supply was lower then, but it doesn't necessarily predict a faster timeline now. You nursed your second child longer and likely had a more robust supply at 14 months than you did at nine months with your first. Every weaning experience is different, even for the same person.

## When to keep an eye on things

Most women who wean abruptly notice significant relief within 7–10 days and feel mostly back to baseline within two to four weeks. But if you develop a hard, painful lump that doesn't soften with gentle hand expression, or if you spike a fever, that's a red flag for mastitis or a plugged duct. Clinical guidance on milk suppression emphasizes watching for mastitis warning signs, especially in the first two weeks after stopping.

If you're still uncomfortably engorged or leaking heavily beyond two weeks, it's worth checking in with your doctor or a lactation consultant. Rarely, persistent milk production can signal a hormonal issue (like [elevated prolactin unrelated to nursing](/articles/can-metformin-help-low-milk-supply-in-pcos/)), but that's uncommon after straightforward weaning.

## What actually helps (and what doesn't)

You're already doing the right things: expressing just enough for comfort (not to empty), using ice to reduce swelling, and giving your body time. Tight-fitting bras or breast binding used to be standard advice, but they don't speed up the process and can increase the risk of plugged ducts.

Some women ask about herbal supplements to dry up milk faster—sage, parsley, and peppermint are the most commonly mentioned. The evidence for these is mostly anecdotal; there aren't rigorous studies showing they significantly accelerate suppression. If you're a week in and already feeling better, you're likely on track without additional intervention.

There *is* a prescription medication (cabergoline) that suppresses prolactin and can stop milk production more quickly, but [it's typically reserved for specific medical situations like bereavement](https://www.hdft.nhs.uk/producing-breastmilk-after-the-loss-of-your-baby/), not routine weaning. It's not something you'd take just to shave a week or two off the timeline.

## The takeaway

Your milk will likely continue to taper off over the next few weeks. You might notice occasional fullness or a small amount of leakage for another week or two, especially in the morning or if you go a long time without expressing anything. That's normal. By the one-month mark, most women who've weaned abruptly feel back to their [pre-nursing baseline](/articles/what-research-says-about-postpartum-return-to-exercise/)—though again, being able to hand-express a tiny amount doesn't mean your supply is still "active."

If you're a week out, less tender, and not needing to pump, you're right on track. The discomfort you had in the first few days was the hardest part. Now it's just a matter of waiting for your body to finish the job.
