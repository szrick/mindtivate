## The problem: a friendship that's all friction

Your daughter's teacher pulls you aside after volunteering in the classroom. There's another first-grader—they can't sit together, can't be partners, but even across the room they're still trying to get each other's attention. Your daughter yells, "Look what I'm doing!" The other girl responds. Neither will let it go. And this isn't new—it's the same girl from preschool and daycare, and the dynamic has followed them into elementary school.

It's confusing because it doesn't look like bullying, exactly. They're drawn to each other. But every teacher who's seen them together says the same thing: this dynamic isn't working.

## Why some peer relationships become more disruptive than supportive

Not every friendship is good for a kid, even when both children seem interested. At six or seven, [children are still learning how to regulate their emotions](/articles/will-preschool-help-my-3-year-old-s-aggression-after-a-new-baby/), read social cues, and know when to walk away. Some pairs just amplify each other's impulsive behavior—one provokes, the other escalates, and neither has the self-control yet to break the cycle.

The [American Academy of Pediatrics notes](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/what-parents-can-do-to-support-friendships.aspx) that parents sometimes need to recognize when an exclusive or intense peer relationship becomes burdensome rather than supportive. That doesn't mean your daughter is a bad kid or the other girl is—it means this particular pairing brings out behavior that neither can manage yet.

Teachers separating them physically is a start, but it's not solving the underlying pull. Your daughter still wants that girl's attention. She's not getting the message that this friendship—as it currently functions—isn't serving her.

## What you can do at home

You can't control what happens across the classroom, but you can coach your daughter on what a good friendship actually feels like. Start by naming what you're seeing without shame: "I noticed that when you and [name] are together, you both get really worked up. Do you feel calm around her, or do you feel like you always have to prove something?"

Six-year-olds don't always have words for this yet, but the question plants a seed. A healthy friendship should feel mostly good—not like a constant contest or a source of trouble.

You can also rehearse what to do when she feels that pull to yell across the room. The StopBullying.gov resource is focused on bullying prevention specifically, but the core de-escalation skills it teaches—treating others respectfully, recognizing when engaging will only make things worse, calmly walking away—apply just as well to non-bullying peer conflict like this one. That's not the same as ignoring someone; it's recognizing when the interaction itself has become the problem.

Role-play it. "What could you do if [name] tries to get your attention during quiet work time?" Let her come up with ideas first (kids are more likely to use strategies they generate themselves), then offer one or two backups: look at your own paper, ask the teacher for help staying focused, move your body so you're not facing her direction.

## When to involve the school more directly

If the pattern continues despite physical separation and your coaching at home, it's worth a deeper conversation with the teacher or school counselor. The [American Academy of Pediatrics guidance on peer problems](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/pages/Problems-With-Peers.aspx?form=HealthyChildren) explains that persistent friendship conflict—especially when it's disrupting learning or causing stress—can warrant involving a school counselor or even your pediatrician.

A counselor can work with both girls (separately or together, depending on the situation) to build skills they're clearly not picking up on their own: reading when someone's annoyed, recognizing their own role in the cycle, practicing self-regulation when they feel that urge to engage.

Some schools also have small social-skills groups for first- and second-graders working on exactly this—learning to make and keep friends without the drama. Ask if that's an option.

## The takeaway

Your daughter isn't making "the wrong friend" in a moral sense—she's caught in a dynamic that neither child knows how to exit. The fact that this pattern has followed them across multiple settings (preschool, daycare, now first grade) suggests it's worth addressing now, while both girls are still young and habits aren't entrenched.

Teach her what good friendships feel like—calm, fun, not constantly getting you in trouble. Give her the language and actions to step back when a peer relationship isn't serving her. And if the pull persists despite everyone's efforts, bring in the school counselor. Sometimes kids need an adult to name what they can't see yet: that walking away from someone isn't mean—it's self-protection.
