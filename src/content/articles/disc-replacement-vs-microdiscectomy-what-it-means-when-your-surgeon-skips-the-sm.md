## Why your surgeon is recommending the bigger procedure

When you've lived with sciatica for four years, tried physical therapy, and had two steroid injections that did nothing, surgery starts to sound reasonable. But hearing that the more conservative option—microdiscectomy—won't work in your case, and that you need to go straight to disc replacement, raises an obvious question: why skip the smaller surgery?

The answer usually comes down to what your surgeon sees on imaging and how your disc has deteriorated. A microdiscectomy removes the herniated portion of the disc that's pressing on your nerve, but it leaves the rest of the disc intact. If that remaining disc is severely degenerated, unstable, or has lost most of its height, your surgeon may predict you'll re-herniate—meaning the problem comes back, sometimes within months. Disc replacement, by contrast, removes the entire damaged disc and replaces it with an artificial one designed to preserve motion at that level.

L5-S1—the lowest disc in your lumbar spine—is a common site for both herniation and degeneration because it bears so much load. It's also one of the levels where artificial disc replacement is FDA-approved and has the most evidence behind it.

## What disc replacement actually involves

Lumbar artificial disc replacement is a bigger operation than microdiscectomy. The surgeon accesses your spine from the front (through your abdomen), removes the entire damaged disc, and inserts a two-part implant that sits between your vertebrae. The goal is to relieve your nerve pain while maintaining movement at that segment, unlike fusion, which locks the vertebrae together.

Recovery is longer—typically several weeks before you're moving comfortably, and three to six months before you're fully healed. The [North American Spine Society's coverage recommendations](https://www.spine.org/Portals/0/assets/downloads/PolicyPractice/CoverageRecommendations/Lumbar-Artificial-Disc-Replacement-DRAFT.pdf) note that candidates for disc replacement generally need to have failed at least six months of nonoperative care (which you have), have a diagnosis confirmed by imaging, and not have certain conditions that would rule it out—things like severe facet joint arthritis, osteoporosis, or prior surgery at that level.

The [FDA patient information for approved lumbar discs](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf12/P120024D.pdf) lays out both the potential benefits—pain relief, preserved motion—and the risks, which include implant migration, wear, adjacent-level degeneration down the line, and the need for revision surgery in some cases. These aren't rare complications, but they're not the norm either; most patients see significant improvement in pain and function.

## What to ask before you commit

Your surgeon's recommendation might be sound, but you deserve to understand the reasoning in detail. Here's what to pin down in your next conversation:

**Why microdiscectomy won't work in your specific case.** Ask to see your MRI images and have your surgeon point out what makes your disc a poor candidate for the smaller procedure. Is it the degree of degeneration? Loss of disc height? Something about the herniation itself?

**What happens if you do nothing.** You've been managing this for four years—what's the trajectory if you don't have surgery? Will the nerve damage that hasn't happened yet become more likely? Or is this about quality of life and pain management?

**What your surgeon's experience is with disc replacement at L5-S1.** How many has he done? What are his complication and revision rates? This isn't about doubting his skill—it's about understanding his track record with this specific procedure.

**What the alternative to disc replacement is.** If disc replacement feels like too much, is fusion an option? Fusion has a longer track record and different trade-offs (no motion at that level, but also no implant wear). Some patients are better candidates for one versus the other.

**What recovery realistically looks like.** Not the best-case scenario—the typical one. How long before you're off pain meds, back to work, able to exercise? What does physical therapy after surgery involve?

## The decision is still yours

A surgeon can tell you what they'd recommend, but they can't tell you what the right choice is for your life. If the sciatica is truly unmanageable and conservative care has failed, surgery makes sense. Whether that surgery is disc replacement, fusion, or—if you want a second opinion—microdiscectomy with the understanding that re-herniation is a risk, depends on your specific anatomy, your surgeon's judgment, and what trade-offs you're willing to accept.

The [clinical guidelines on lumbar disc herniation with radiculopathy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24239490/?dopt=Abstract) emphasize shared decision-making: your values, your tolerance for risk, and your goals for function all matter as much as what the imaging shows. If something about this recommendation doesn't sit right, or if you want to hear a different perspective, a second opinion from another spine surgeon is completely reasonable. You're the one who lives with the outcome.
