## The expectation that you'll cry

You've heard the stories. Other parents talk about sobbing when their baby got shots, feeling devastated watching the needle go in, needing to leave the room. So when your baby got vaccinated and you felt… calm? Focused on soothing them? You might be wondering if something's off.

It's not. The idea that a "good" parent cries during their baby's vaccinations is a story we tell ourselves about what love is supposed to look like—but it doesn't match how attachment or caregiving actually works.

## Why some parents cry and others don't

Your emotional response to your baby's shots isn't a referendum on how much you love them. It's shaped by a mix of factors: your own pain tolerance and how you were taught to respond to medical procedures as a kid, your general anxiety levels, how much sleep you're getting, whether this is your first baby or your third.

Some parents have a visceral, empathetic response to seeing their child in pain—even brief pain. Others immediately shift into problem-solving mode. Neither response is more loving. [Building a relationship with your baby](https://cambspborochildrenshealth.nhs.uk/behaviour-emotions-and-mental-health/building-a-relationship-with-your-baby/) happens through everyday interactions—feeding, diaper changes, soothing when they're fussy—not through whether you cry at the pediatrician's office.

What your baby actually needs during a shot is exactly what you described doing: helping them calm down. The CDC recommends comforting babies through [cuddling, a soothing voice, and eye contact](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines-children/before-during-after-shots/index.html) during vaccination. Your baby doesn't need you to cry. They need you to be present and responsive.

## When calm feels like coldness

The worry underneath your question might be less about the shots themselves and more about whether your overall emotional response to parenthood matches what you expected. If you're consistently feeling detached—not just calm during stressful moments, but genuinely disconnected from your baby day to day—that's worth paying attention to.

But there's a big difference between "I didn't cry during a two-second needle stick because I knew it was necessary and brief" and "I feel numb or disconnected from my baby most of the time." The first is a reasonable response to a routine medical procedure. The second might be a sign of a postpartum mood disorder—whether depression, anxiety, or something else—and deserves support.

If you're questioning your bond because you didn't have one specific emotional reaction, it's worth remembering that bonding looks different for everyone and develops over time. Some parents feel an immediate rush of love; others grow into it over weeks or months. Both are normal. That said, if you're still feeling disconnected after several months, talking to your provider can help you figure out what's going on.

## What actually signals a problem

Not crying at shots? Not a problem. But persistent emotional flatness, difficulty feeling joy or connection, or a sense that you're just going through the motions without really feeling present—those are signs that talking to your doctor or a therapist would help.

One thing worth mentioning separately: intrusive thoughts about your baby being harmed can be frightening, but they're actually common and don't mean you're dangerous. They're often a feature of [postpartum anxiety or OCD](/articles/postpartum-rage-the-symptoms-no-one-prepares-you-for/) rather than depression. If you're experiencing them, that alone is reason to reach out for support—not because something is wrong with you, but because treatment helps and you don't have to white-knuckle through it.

The distinction matters because parenting culture already heaps so much judgment on mothers for every tiny choice or reaction. You don't need to add "didn't cry enough at the pediatrician" to the list of things you're monitoring for evidence that you're doing it wrong.

## The takeaway

Your baby needed their shots. You understood that, stayed calm, and helped them feel better afterward. That's not coldness—it's exactly the kind of steady, responsive caregiving that builds security. The tears other parents shed aren't wrong either, but they're not the benchmark for love.

If you're feeling connected to your baby in the everyday moments—when you're feeding them, watching them discover their hands, soothing them to sleep—then you're doing fine. And if you're not feeling that connection yet, or if you're feeling overwhelmed or detached more broadly, reach out. But the shots? You handled them exactly right.
