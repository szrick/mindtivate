## The urgency is real—and so is the overwhelm

You've just learned your mother has primary progressive aphasia, a form of dementia that specifically attacks the language centers of the brain. Over time, she'll lose the ability to speak, understand words, read, and write. The diagnosis comes with a ticking clock, and you're trying to figure out what questions to ask while there's still time. You've started a list—letters for milestone birthdays, something to read at your wedding—but you're stuck, wondering what else you should capture before her words disappear.

This is one of the hardest positions to be in. You're [grieving a loss that hasn't fully happened yet](/articles/what-to-say-when-you-learn-about-a-death-months-later/), while also trying to be strategic and thoughtful about preserving what you can. There's no perfect list, but there are categories of questions that tend to matter most when someone's ability to communicate is on a timeline.

## Why language loss makes this so urgent

[Primary progressive aphasia](https://www.asha.org/slp/explaining-primary-progressive-aphasia/) is different from typical Alzheimer's dementia. Your mother's memory may stay relatively intact for a while, but her ability to find words, form sentences, and understand language will decline progressively. That means the window for verbal conversation is narrower than it would be with other forms of dementia, where communication challenges come later.

The National Institute on Aging emphasizes starting these conversations early after a dementia diagnosis—not just about care preferences, but about values, stories, and the things that matter most. Once aphasia progresses, even yes/no questions become difficult.

## What to ask: categories that matter

**Her own story, in her words.** Ask about her childhood, her parents, what she remembers about becoming a mother for the first time. What was she like at your age? What did she worry about? What made her feel brave? These aren't just facts—they're the texture of who she is, and they're often the stories that don't come up in everyday conversation.

**The practical stuff you'll need later.** Family recipes (not just ingredients, but the little tricks she does without thinking). Where important documents are. Passwords, account details, the name of the neighbor who has a spare key. It's not romantic, but you'll be grateful to have it.

**What she wants you to know about hard times.** If there were family rifts, estrangements, or painful chapters, now is the time to ask what she'd want you to understand about them. Not to reopen wounds, but so you're not left guessing later about why certain people drifted or what really happened.

**Her hopes and worries for you.** What does she hope your life looks like in ten years? What does she worry about for you? What does she wish she'd done differently as a parent? These can be hard to hear, but they're also often the conversations people most wish they'd had.

**Messages for specific moments.** You've already thought of this—letters for milestone birthdays, something for your wedding. Expand it: what would she want to tell a future grandchild? What would she say if you called her after a really hard day, five years from now? What would she want you to remember when you're doubting yourself?

**The small things that make her *her*.** What's her favorite memory of the two of you? What song makes her think of you? If she could describe you in three words, what would they be? What's something she's never told you but always wanted to?

## How to actually do this without it feeling like an interrogation

Don't try to get everything in one sitting. Spread these conversations out. Some will happen naturally; others you'll need to prompt. 

Record everything—audio or video, with her permission. You'll want to hear her voice later, and details you think you'll remember will blur over time. Write things down too, in case technology fails or you want something you can hold.

Communication strategies for dementia emphasize giving the person time to respond, asking one question at a time, and not rushing. As her aphasia progresses, you may need to shift to yes/no questions or offer choices rather than open-ended prompts. But right now, while she can still form sentences, let her talk. Don't interrupt to correct details or fill in words she's searching for unless she asks.

## What this won't fix—and what it will

Capturing your mother's words won't make the loss easier. It won't stop the progression of her illness or give you back the conversations you'll want to have with her in five or ten years. It won't feel like enough, because it isn't—there's no amount of questions that will feel like enough time.

But it will give you something to return to. A record of who she was when she could still tell you herself. Her voice, her phrasing, the way she explains things. It's not a replacement for her presence, but it's a thread back to it. And when language is gone, that thread will matter more than you can imagine right now.
