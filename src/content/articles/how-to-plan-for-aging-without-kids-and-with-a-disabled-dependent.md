## The question that keeps you up at night

You're 35, your nephew Ross is 10, and you've already promised yourself he'll never live in an institution. You're switching to nursing, you volunteer in palliative care, and you've seen enough hospital rooms to know exactly what the question is: when you're 85 and Ross is 60, who helps you both?

It's not morbid to ask. It's practical. And you're far from alone—about 23% of adults in their 50s and 22% of those in their 60s have never had children, and that number is rising. When you add a disabled dependent to the picture, the planning gets more urgent, not less.

## Why "solo aging" planning is different (and more critical)

Most retirement advice assumes you'll have adult children to coordinate care, make medical decisions, or at least answer the phone when the hospital calls. When that's not your reality—whether by choice, circumstance, or because you're the one doing the caregiving—you need a different framework.

The term "solo ager" applies to anyone aging without a spouse, partner, or adult children nearby to rely on for support. Your situation is more complex: you're planning not just for yourself, but for Ross, whose care needs may outlast your ability to provide them. That means your plan has to account for two people's long-term care, two sets of legal documents, and a support network robust enough to handle both.

Long-term care includes everything from help with daily activities like bathing and dressing to skilled nursing care—and most people reaching 65 will need some form of it. For solo agers, the question isn't whether you'll need it, but who will arrange it and how you'll pay for it.

## The legal scaffolding you need in place now

Before you're 85—ideally, before you're 45—you need a set of legal documents that answer the "who" question for both you and Ross. These aren't just nice-to-haves; they're the difference between your wishes being honored and a court-appointed stranger making decisions.

Start with three core documents for yourself: a **medical power of attorney** (also called a health care proxy), an **advance directive** (living will), and a **financial power of attorney**. The medical POA designates someone to make health decisions if you can't. The advance directive spells out what kinds of treatment you want. The financial POA gives someone authority to manage your money and property if you're incapacitated.

For Ross, the planning is more involved. If your sister is still living, she likely holds guardianship now, but you'll need a clear legal pathway for that responsibility to transfer to you when the time comes. If Ross is unable to make his own medical or financial decisions, you'll need guardianship or conservatorship paperwork in place—and a backup plan if something happens to you before he does. Legal and financial planning should start well before a crisis, not during one.

The hard part: choosing who to name in those documents when you don't have kids. A sibling? A younger cousin? A trusted friend? Whoever you pick needs to be willing, capable, and ideally younger than you—because they'll age too. Some solo agers turn to professional fiduciaries, especially for financial management, rather than burdening a peer.

## Building a support network that isn't family

Legal documents answer "who decides," but they don't answer "who shows up." Who drives you to a procedure? Who checks in when you're recovering? Who notices if Ross isn't doing well?

One model gaining traction is the **Village Movement**—community-based, member-driven nonprofits where neighbors help each other age in place. Members pay an annual fee and get access to volunteer services (rides, home repairs, social events) and vetted service providers. It's not a physical place; it's a network. The first village launched in Boston in 2002, and there are now 280 across the U.S., with more forming.

Villages won't replace a nursing home if you need skilled care, but they can delay that transition by solving transportation problems, reducing isolation, and connecting you to local resources. For someone planning to care for a disabled adult relative, a village could be the safety net that keeps both of you in the community longer. You can search for one near you through the Village to Village Network or ask at your local Area Agency on Aging—there are [more than 600 federally funded agencies](https://acl.gov/help) that coordinate aging and disability services.

If there's no village near you, consider building your own smaller version: a core group of friends, neighbors, or fellow caregivers who commit to checking in, sharing resources, and stepping up in a pinch. It sounds informal, but research on villages shows that even modest increases in perceived social support significantly boost confidence in aging at home.

## The financial realities no one wants to talk about

You're not raising a child, so you're not spending $230,000 on childhood expenses—but you may be spending far more than that on long-term care. A year of in-home care averages over $60,000; assisted living runs higher; skilled nursing care higher still. And here's the kicker: [Medicare generally does not cover custodial long-term care](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/long-term-care)—the help with daily activities that most people eventually need.

That means you need a plan to pay for it. Long-term care insurance is one option, though it's expensive and harder to qualify for as you age. Some solo agers max out retirement contributions with the assumption that a significant portion will go toward care, not leisure. Others look into continuing care retirement communities (CCRCs), which offer independent living, assisted living, and nursing care on one campus in exchange for a large upfront payment and monthly fees.

For Ross, the financial picture is different. If he qualifies for Medicaid or disability benefits, some of his care costs may be covered—but navigating that system is its own job, and you'll need to understand how your financial support interacts with his eligibility. An elder law or disability attorney can help structure things so you're not accidentally disqualifying him from benefits he's entitled to.

## What to do this year

You don't need to solve everything at 35, but you do need to start. Here's what moves the needle:

- **Get the legal documents drafted.** If you don't have a medical POA, advance directive, and financial POA for yourself, make appointments with an estate attorney this quarter. Ask about guardianship planning for Ross at the same time.
- **Start the money conversation.** Meet with a financial planner who understands solo aging and disability planning. Ask about long-term care insurance, how much to save, and how to structure assets so Ross's care is funded without jeopardizing his benefits.
- **Build your network now, not later.** Join or start a village. Deepen friendships with people who could be part of your safety net. If you're switching to nursing, you're already embedded in a caregiving community—lean into those relationships.
- **Write down your plan.** Not just the legal stuff, but the practical stuff: where you want to live as you age, what kind of care you want Ross to receive, who you trust to make decisions. Share it with the people you're counting on.

You're asking the right question at the right time. Most people don't think about this until they're already in crisis. You have decades to build something solid—and that's exactly what Ross (and your 85-year-old self) will need.
